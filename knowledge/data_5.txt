Human anatomy is the study of the structure of the human body, while physiology is the study of how the body functions. The human body is organized hierarchically, from atoms and molecules to cells, tissues, organs, organ systems, and the whole organism. There are eleven major organ systems in the human body. The integumentary system includes the skin, hair, nails, and associated glands. The skin is the largest organ of the body, accounting for about fifteen percent of total body weight. It has three main layers. The epidermis is the outermost layer, composed of stratified squamous epithelium. The outermost part of the epidermis, the stratum corneum, consists of dead keratinized cells that provide a waterproof barrier. Melanocytes in the epidermis produce melanin, the pigment that protects against ultraviolet radiation and determines skin color. The dermis lies beneath the epidermis and contains connective tissue, blood vessels, nerve endings, hair follicles, and glands. Sebaceous glands secrete sebum, an oily substance that lubricates skin and hair. Sweat glands, or sudoriferous glands, produce sweat for thermoregulation. The hypodermis, or subcutaneous layer, is not technically part of the skin but lies beneath the dermis. It consists of loose connective tissue and adipose tissue, providing insulation, energy storage, and cushioning. The skeletal system provides structural support, protection for internal organs, attachment points for muscles, storage of calcium and phosphorus, and production of blood cells in the bone marrow. The adult human skeleton has two hundred six bones. The axial skeleton includes the skull, vertebral column, ribs, and sternum. The skull consists of twenty two bones, including eight cranial bones that protect the brain and fourteen facial bones. The vertebral column, or spine, is composed of thirty three vertebrae. Seven cervical vertebrae form the neck, twelve thoracic vertebrae articulate with the ribs, five lumbar vertebrae support the lower back, five sacral vertebrae are fused into the sacrum, and four coccygeal vertebrae are fused into the coccyx or tailbone. Intervertebral discs of fibrocartilage separate the vertebrae and act as shock absorbers. The appendicular skeleton includes the bones of the limbs and the girdles that attach them to the axial skeleton. The pectoral girdle consists of the clavicles and scapulae and attaches the upper limbs. Each upper limb has a humerus in the arm, radius and ulna in the forearm, eight carpal bones in the wrist, five metacarpals in the hand, and fourteen phalanges in the fingers. The pelvic girdle, or hip bones, attaches the lower limbs. Each lower limb has a femur in the thigh, the longest and strongest bone in the body, a patella or kneecap, tibia and fibula in the leg, seven tarsal bones in the ankle, five metatarsals in the foot, and fourteen phalanges in the toes. Joints, or articulations, are where two or more bones meet. Fibrous joints, such as the sutures of the skull, are immovable. Cartilaginous joints, such as the intervertebral discs, are slightly movable. Synovial joints are freely movable and contain synovial fluid for lubrication. Types of synovial joints include ball and socket joints such as the shoulder and hip, hinge joints such as the elbow and knee, pivot joints such as the joint between the first and second vertebrae, condyloid joints such as the wrist, saddle joints such as the thumb, and gliding joints such as the intercarpal joints. The muscular system consists of over six hundred skeletal muscles that produce voluntary movement. Skeletal muscle is striated and under conscious control. Each muscle is made of fascicles, which contain muscle fibers, which are individual muscle cells. Muscle fibers contain myofibrils, which are composed of repeating units called sarcomeres. Sarcomeres contain the proteins actin and myosin, which slide past each other during contraction according to the sliding filament theory. Contraction is initiated by a nerve impulse that releases acetylcholine at the neuromuscular junction, leading to depolarization of the muscle fiber membrane and release of calcium ions from the sarcoplasmic reticulum. Calcium binds to troponin, causing tropomyosin to move and expose binding sites on actin, allowing myosin heads to attach and pull. Major skeletal muscles include the deltoid of the shoulder, biceps brachii and triceps brachii of the arm, pectoralis major of the chest, rectus abdominis of the abdomen, quadriceps femoris group of the thigh, hamstrings group of the back of the thigh, gastrocnemius of the calf, and gluteus maximus of the buttock. Smooth muscle is non striated and involuntary, found in the walls of hollow organs such as the stomach, intestines, bladder, and blood vessels. Cardiac muscle is striated but involuntary, found only in the heart, with intercalated discs that allow rapid spread of electrical signals. The nervous system controls and coordinates body functions. It is divided into the central nervous system, consisting of the brain and spinal cord, and the peripheral nervous system, consisting of nerves that connect the central nervous system to the rest of the body. Neurons are the functional units of the nervous system. A typical neuron has a cell body containing the nucleus, dendrites that receive signals, and an axon that transmits signals to other neurons, muscles, or glands. The axon is often covered by a myelin sheath, produced by Schwann cells in the peripheral nervous system and oligodendrocytes in the central nervous system, which insulates the axon and speeds signal conduction. Neurons communicate at synapses. When an action potential reaches the axon terminal, it triggers the release of neurotransmitters from synaptic vesicles into the synaptic cleft. Neurotransmitters bind to receptors on the postsynaptic membrane, causing ion channels to open and generating either an excitatory or inhibitory postsynaptic potential. Common neurotransmitters include acetylcholine, dopamine, serotonin, norepinephrine, gamma aminobutyric acid or GABA, and glutamate. The brain is the most complex organ in the human body, containing approximately eighty six billion neurons. It is divided into several major regions. The cerebrum is the largest part, responsible for conscious thought, memory, sensation, and voluntary movement. It is divided into left and right hemispheres connected by the corpus callosum. Each hemisphere has four lobes. The frontal lobe controls executive functions, planning, personality, and voluntary movement. The parietal lobe processes touch, pressure, temperature, and pain. The temporal lobe processes hearing, memory, and language comprehension. The occipital lobe processes vision. The cerebral cortex is the outer layer of the cerebrum, composed of gray matter, which contains neuron cell bodies. Beneath the cortex is white matter, composed of myelinated axons. The cerebellum is located at the back of the brain and coordinates movement, balance, and posture. The brainstem connects the brain to the spinal cord and controls basic life functions such as breathing, heart rate, and blood pressure. It consists of the midbrain, pons, and medulla oblongata. The limbic system, including the hippocampus, amygdala, and hypothalamus, processes emotion, memory, and motivation. The hypothalamus also regulates body temperature, hunger, thirst, and the endocrine system. The spinal cord runs through the vertebral canal and transmits signals between the brain and the periphery. It also mediates reflex arcs, which are rapid, automatic responses to stimuli that do not require conscious brain involvement. The patellar reflex, or knee jerk reflex, is an example. The peripheral nervous system includes cranial nerves, which emerge directly from the brain, and spinal nerves, which emerge from the spinal cord. The peripheral nervous system is divided into the somatic nervous system, which controls voluntary movement and transmits sensory information, and the autonomic nervous system, which controls involuntary functions. The autonomic nervous system has two divisions. The sympathetic nervous system prepares the body for stress or emergency, the fight or flight response, increasing heart rate, dilating pupils, and redirecting blood flow to muscles. The parasympathetic nervous system promotes rest and digestion, the rest and digest response, slowing heart rate and increasing digestive activity. The endocrine system consists of glands that secrete hormones directly into the bloodstream. Hormones are chemical messengers that regulate growth, metabolism, reproduction, and homeostasis. The hypothalamus, located in the brain, controls the pituitary gland. The pituitary gland, often called the master gland, produces hormones that regulate other endocrine glands. Growth hormone promotes growth of bones and tissues. Thyroid stimulating hormone stimulates the thyroid gland. Adrenocorticotropic hormone stimulates the adrenal cortex. Prolactin stimulates milk production. Antidiuretic hormone, or ADH, regulates water reabsorption by the kidneys. Oxytocin stimulates uterine contractions during childbirth and milk ejection during breastfeeding. The thyroid gland, located in the neck, produces thyroxine and triiodothyronine, which regulate metabolic rate. Calcitonin lowers blood calcium levels. The parathyroid glands, embedded in the thyroid, produce parathyroid hormone, which raises blood calcium levels. The adrenal glands sit on top of the kidneys. The adrenal cortex produces cortisol, which regulates metabolism and stress response, and aldosterone, which regulates sodium and potassium balance. The adrenal medulla produces epinephrine and norepinephrine, which mediate the fight or flight response. The pancreas produces insulin and glucagon. Insulin lowers blood glucose by promoting uptake into cells and storage as glycogen. Glucagon raises blood glucose by promoting breakdown of glycogen and production of glucose from amino acids. The gonads produce sex hormones. The ovaries produce estrogen and progesterone, which regulate the menstrual cycle and female secondary sexual characteristics. The testes produce testosterone, which regulates sperm production and male secondary sexual characteristics. The pineal gland produces melatonin, which regulates circadian rhythms. The cardiovascular system transports oxygen, nutrients, hormones, and waste products throughout the body. It consists of the heart, blood vessels, and blood. The heart is a four chambered muscular organ located in the mediastinum. The two upper chambers are the atria, which receive blood. The two lower chambers are the ventricles, which pump blood out of the heart. The right side of the heart pumps deoxygenated blood to the lungs via the pulmonary artery. The left side pumps oxygenated blood to the body via the aorta. The cardiac cycle consists of systole, the contraction phase when blood is ejected, and diastole, the relaxation phase when the chambers fill with blood. The sinoatrial node, or SA node, the natural pacemaker of the heart, generates electrical impulses that spread through the atria, causing them to contract. The impulse then reaches the atrioventricular node, or AV node, which delays it slightly before passing through the bundle of His and Purkinje fibers, causing the ventricles to contract. An electrocardiogram, or ECG, records the electrical activity of the heart. Blood vessels include arteries, which carry blood away from the heart; arterioles, which are small branches of arteries; capillaries, where exchange of oxygen, carbon dioxide, nutrients, and waste occurs; venules, which collect blood from capillaries; and veins, which carry blood back to the heart. Arteries have thick muscular walls to withstand high pressure. Veins have thinner walls and contain valves to prevent backflow because venous pressure is low. Blood is a connective tissue composed of plasma and formed elements. Plasma is about ninety two percent water and contains proteins, electrolytes, nutrients, gases, and waste products. The formed elements include red blood cells, or erythrocytes; white blood cells, or leukocytes; and platelets, or thrombocytes. Red blood cells are biconcave discs that lack nuclei in mammals. They contain hemoglobin, a protein with iron that binds oxygen. Each red blood cell contains about two hundred seventy million hemoglobin molecules, and each hemoglobin molecule can carry four oxygen molecules. Red blood cells are produced in bone marrow through erythropoiesis and have a lifespan of about one hundred twenty days. White blood cells are part of the immune system. Granulocytes, including neutrophils, eosinophils, and basophils, contain granules in their cytoplasm. Agranulocytes, including lymphocytes and monocytes, do not. Neutrophils are the most abundant white blood cell and are the first responders to bacterial infection. Lymphocytes include B cells, which produce antibodies, and T cells, which kill infected cells and regulate immune responses. Monocytes become macrophages that engulf pathogens and debris. Platelets are cell fragments that form blood clots. When a blood vessel is damaged, platelets adhere to the exposed collagen and release chemicals that activate the coagulation cascade. Thrombin converts fibrinogen into fibrin, forming a mesh that traps red blood cells and platelets, creating a clot. Blood types are determined by the presence or absence of A and B antigens on red blood cells. Type A has A antigens, type B has B antigens, type AB has both, and type O has neither. The Rh factor is another antigen. Rh positive individuals have the Rh antigen, while Rh negative individuals do not. Blood transfusion must match compatible types to avoid transfusion reactions. The lymphatic system is a network of vessels, nodes, and organs that returns excess interstitial fluid to the bloodstream, transports fats from the digestive system, and defends against pathogens. Lymph is the fluid that circulates in the lymphatic system. Lymph nodes filter lymph and contain lymphocytes that respond to pathogens. The spleen filters blood, removes old red blood cells, and mounts immune responses. The thymus is where T lymphocytes mature. Tonsils and adenoids trap pathogens entering through the mouth and nose. The respiratory system provides oxygen to the blood and removes carbon dioxide. Air enters through the nose or mouth, passes through the pharynx, larynx, trachea, bronchi, bronchioles, and finally reaches the alveoli, which are tiny air sacs in the lungs. The larynx contains the vocal cords, which vibrate to produce sound. The trachea is reinforced with C shaped cartilage rings to keep it open. The lungs are paired organs in the thoracic cavity. The right lung has three lobes, and the left lung has two lobes to accommodate the heart. Gas exchange occurs in the alveoli. Oxygen diffuses from alveolar air into the blood in pulmonary capillaries, while carbon dioxide diffuses from blood into the alveoli to be exhaled. Oxygen is carried in the blood primarily bound to hemoglobin. Carbon dioxide is transported as bicarbonate ions, dissolved in plasma, or bound to hemoglobin. Breathing, or ventilation, is driven by the diaphragm and intercostal muscles. Inhalation occurs when the diaphragm contracts and flattens, increasing thoracic volume and decreasing pressure, drawing air in. Exhalation is normally passive as the diaphragm relaxes, decreasing volume and increasing pressure, pushing air out. The respiratory center in the medulla oblongata regulates breathing rate based on blood carbon dioxide and pH levels. The digestive system breaks down food into absorbable nutrients. The alimentary canal is a long tube from mouth to anus, including the mouth, pharynx, esophagus, stomach, small intestine, large intestine, rectum, and anus. Accessory organs include the salivary glands, liver, gallbladder, and pancreas. Digestion begins in the mouth, where teeth chew food and salivary amylase begins breaking down starch. The tongue shapes food into a bolus for swallowing. The bolus passes through the pharynx and esophagus via peristalsis, rhythmic waves of muscle contraction. The lower esophageal sphincter opens to allow food into the stomach. The stomach secretes gastric juice containing hydrochloric acid and pepsinogen. Hydrochloric acid kills bacteria and converts pepsinogen into pepsin, which breaks down proteins. The stomach churns food into chyme. The pyloric sphincter regulates release of chyme into the small intestine. The small intestine is about six meters long and is the primary site of digestion and absorption. It has three segments: the duodenum, jejunum, and ileum. The duodenum receives chyme from the stomach, bile from the liver and gallbladder, and pancreatic juice from the pancreas. Bile emulsifies fats, breaking them into small droplets. Pancreatic juice contains enzymes that digest carbohydrates, proteins, and fats. The intestinal lining has fingerlike projections called villi, and each villus has microvilli, forming a brush border that increases surface area for absorption. Nutrients are absorbed into blood capillaries and lacteals, lymphatic vessels that absorb fats. The large intestine, or colon, absorbs water and electrolytes, compacts waste into feces, and houses beneficial bacteria. The appendix is a small pouch attached to the cecum, the beginning of the large intestine. Feces are stored in the rectum and eliminated through the anus via two sphincters, one involuntary and one voluntary. The liver is the largest internal organ. It performs over five hundred functions, including producing bile, metabolizing carbohydrates, proteins, and fats, detoxifying harmful substances, synthesizing blood proteins, storing glycogen, iron, and vitamins, and recycling red blood cells. The gallbladder stores and concentrates bile. The pancreas produces digestive enzymes and bicarbonate for the small intestine and secretes insulin and glucagon into the blood. The urinary system removes waste products from the blood and regulates water and electrolyte balance. It consists of the kidneys, ureters, urinary bladder, and urethra. The kidneys are bean shaped organs located retroperitoneally, just below the rib cage. Each kidney contains about one million functional units called nephrons. Each nephron consists of a renal corpuscle, which includes the glomerulus a tuft of capillaries and Bowman's capsule, and a renal tubule. Blood enters the glomerulus under pressure, forcing water and small solutes into Bowman's capsule to form filtrate. The filtrate passes through the proximal convoluted tubule, loop of Henle, distal convoluted tubule, and collecting duct. As the filtrate passes, water, glucose, amino acids, and ions are reabsorbed into the blood, while waste products such as urea, creatinine, and excess ions are secreted into the filtrate. The final product, urine, drains from the collecting ducts into the renal pelvis, then into the ureter. Ureters transport urine to the urinary bladder, where it is stored. When the bladder is full, stretch receptors signal the brain, and urination occurs through the urethra. The reproductive system produces gametes and sex hormones. The male reproductive system includes the testes, epididymis, vas deferens, seminal vesicles, prostate gland, bulbourethral glands, and penis. The testes, located in the scrotum outside the body cavity, produce sperm through spermatogenesis and produce testosterone. Sperm mature and are stored in the epididymis. During ejaculation, sperm travel through the vas deferens, mix with seminal fluid from the seminal vesicles and prostate gland to form semen, and are expelled through the urethra. The female reproductive system includes the ovaries, fallopian tubes, uterus, cervix, vagina, and external genitalia. The ovaries produce eggs through oogenesis and produce estrogen and progesterone. Approximately every twenty eight days, an egg is released from an ovary in a process called ovulation. The egg travels through the fallopian tube, where fertilization may occur. If fertilized, the embryo implants in the endometrium, the lining of the uterus, and pregnancy begins. If not fertilized, the endometrium is shed during menstruation. The vagina is the birth canal and receives the penis during intercourse. The immune system defends the body against pathogens, which include bacteria, viruses, fungi, protozoa, and parasites. Innate immunity is present from birth and provides immediate, nonspecific defense. Physical barriers include skin and mucous membranes. Chemical barriers include stomach acid, antimicrobial enzymes in tears and saliva, and antimicrobial peptides. Cellular defenses include phagocytes such as neutrophils and macrophages, natural killer cells, and dendritic cells. Inflammation is a nonspecific response to tissue damage or infection, characterized by redness, heat, swelling, and pain. Adaptive immunity is specific and develops after exposure to a pathogen. It has two branches. Humoral immunity involves B cells that produce antibodies. Antibodies are proteins that bind to specific antigens on pathogens, marking them for destruction. Cellular immunity involves T cells. Helper T cells activate other immune cells. Cytotoxic T cells kill infected cells. Memory B cells and memory T cells provide long lasting immunity, allowing a faster and stronger response upon reexposure to the same pathogen. Vaccination works by exposing the immune system to a harmless form of a pathogen or its antigens, inducing immunological memory without causing disease. Autoimmune diseases occur when the immune system attacks the body's own tissues, such as in rheumatoid arthritis, type one diabetes, and multiple sclerosis. Allergies occur when the immune system overreacts to harmless environmental antigens called allergens. Human development begins with fertilization, when a sperm fuses with an egg to form a zygote. The zygote undergoes cleavage, forming a morula, then a blastocyst. The blastocyst implants in the uterine wall. The inner cell mass becomes the embryo, while the outer trophoblast becomes the placenta. Gastrulation forms three germ layers. The ectoderm gives rise to the nervous system, skin, and hair. The mesoderm gives rise to muscles, bones, blood, and the circulatory system. The endoderm gives rise to the lining of the digestive tract, lungs, and many internal organs. Organogenesis is the formation of organs. The embryonic period lasts from week two to week eight. The fetal period lasts from week nine to birth, which typically occurs around forty weeks after the last menstrual period. Aging is a complex process involving progressive decline in physiological function. Cellular theories of aging include telomere shortening, where the protective caps on chromosomes shorten with each cell division; oxidative damage from reactive oxygen species; and accumulation of damaged proteins and DNA. Lifestyle factors that influence health and longevity include nutrition, exercise, sleep, stress management, and avoidance of smoking and excessive alcohol consumption. Common causes of death globally include cardiovascular disease, cancer, respiratory infections, stroke, and dementia. Medical imaging techniques allow visualization of the body's interior. X rays use ionizing radiation to produce images of bones and dense structures. Computed tomography or CT scans combine multiple X rays to create cross sectional images. Magnetic resonance imaging or MRI uses strong magnetic fields and radio waves to image soft tissues in detail. Ultrasound uses high frequency sound waves to image fetuses and soft tissues. Positron emission tomography or PET scans detect metabolic activity using radioactive tracers. Pharmacology is the study of drugs and their effects on the body. Drugs can act as agonists, which activate receptors, or antagonists, which block receptors. Pharmacokinetics describes how the body absorbs, distributes, metabolizes, and excretes a drug. Pharmacodynamics describes how a drug affects the body. Antibiotics kill bacteria or inhibit their growth. Antivirals treat viral infections. Analgesics relieve pain. Anesthetics cause loss of sensation. Antihypertensives lower blood pressure. Antidepressants treat depression. Anticoagulants prevent blood clots. Statins lower cholesterol. Chemotherapy drugs kill rapidly dividing cancer cells. Clinical trials are research studies that test the safety and effectiveness of new medical treatments. They proceed through phases. Phase one tests safety in a small number of healthy volunteers. Phase two tests effectiveness and side effects in a larger group of patients.
