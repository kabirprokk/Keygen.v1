Human language is not just about words and grammar. It is about meaning, context, intention, and social relationship. To talk like a human, an AI must learn pragmatics, which is the study of how context contributes to meaning. Pragmatics goes beyond the literal dictionary definition of words. For example, if someone says it is cold in here, the literal meaning is a statement about temperature. But in conversation, that same sentence might be a request to close a window, a hint that the speaker wants to leave, a complaint about the heating system, or even an indirect criticism of the person who left the door open. Humans understand these indirect meanings automatically because they consider the speaker, the listener, the setting, and the shared knowledge between them. The cooperative principle, introduced by philosopher Paul Grice, describes how humans communicate efficiently. Grice proposed four maxims that people follow in conversation. The maxim of quantity says to give the right amount of information, not too little and not too much. If someone asks what time it is, saying it is daytime is unhelpfully too little, while saying the time down to the millisecond including the history of timekeeping is too much. The maxim of quality says to be truthful and not say things you know to be false or lack evidence for. The maxim of relation says to be relevant to the topic of conversation. If someone asks where the nearest coffee shop is, answering that the sky is blue violates the maxim of relation. The maxim of manner says to be clear, orderly, and avoid ambiguity. Humans routinely violate these maxims on purpose to create humor, sarcasm, irony, or politeness. When a maxim is violated, the listener infers a conversational implicature, an unstated meaning that the speaker intends. For example, if a job interviewer asks about a candidate's punctuality and the response is well, he has never been late for lunch, the speaker violates the maxim of quantity by not giving direct information about work punctuality, but the listener infers that the candidate might be unreliable for work. Understanding this kind of inference is central to human conversation. Humans also use politeness strategies to avoid threatening each other's face. Face is the public self image that every person wants to maintain. Positive face is the desire to be liked, approved of, and appreciated. Negative face is the desire to be free from imposition and not burdened by others. When making a request, humans often soften it with hedges, like could you possibly or if it is not too much trouble, to protect the listener's negative face. When disagreeing, humans might first agree partially, like I see what you mean but, to protect the listener's positive face. Understanding face and politeness helps an AI avoid sounding rude or robotic. Question understanding requires recognizing not just the literal words but the question type and the intent behind it. Questions are categorized by their structure and expected answer. Yes no questions expect a confirmation or denial. Do you like coffee expects yes or no. Wh questions begin with who, what, where, when, why, or how and expect open ended information. What is your name expects a name. Where are you from expects a place. Why questions ask for reasons or explanations. How questions ask for methods, conditions, or descriptions. Alternative questions offer choices and expect the listener to pick one. Do you want tea or coffee expects tea or coffee not a yes or no. Tag questions attach a short question to a declarative sentence, like it is cold outside is not it, and they express uncertainty or seek agreement. Rhetorical questions are not meant to be answered at all. Who knows, said in frustration, means nobody knows. Rhetorical questions make a point rather than request information. Leading questions suggest their own answer. You did not take the last cookie, did you, subtly pressures the listener to agree. Understanding the difference between sincere questions and rhetorical or leading questions is crucial for natural dialogue. Humans also use indirect questions. Instead of asking can you pass the salt, they might say the salt is over there, which is a statement that functions as a request. Instead of asking will you come to my party, they might say I am having a party on Saturday, which is an announcement that functions as an invitation. An AI that takes every statement literally will miss these pragmatic functions. Answer formulation is equally complex. A good answer is not always a direct answer. Humans consider what the asker already knows, what they need to know, and the social relationship. If someone asks how much money you make, a direct answer might be inappropriate. Humans might deflect, give a vague range, say I would rather not say, or respond with a question like why do you ask. If someone asks a question based on a false assumption, a good answer corrects the assumption first. For example, why did you stop smoking, if the person never smoked, the correct response is I have never smoked, which indirectly points out the false presupposition. Humans also use repair strategies when communication breaks down. If a listener does not understand a question, they might ask for clarification, like what do you mean or could you rephrase that. If a speaker is not understood, they might repeat louder, rephrase in simpler words, or provide an example. Grounding is the process of establishing shared understanding. Humans use backchannel responses like uh huh, okay, I see, nodding, and yeah to show they are following along without interrupting. These tiny signals are essential for smooth conversation. An AI that stays silent while processing will feel unnatural. Pauses and fillers like um, uh, well, you know, and like also serve conversational functions. Fillers signal that the speaker is not finished, is thinking, or is about to say something delicate. Humans do not speak in perfectly planned sentences. They start a sentence, correct themselves, interrupt themselves, trail off, and restart. An AI that speaks only in perfect, complete sentences will sound like a textbook, not a person. Psycholinguistics is the study of how humans produce and understand language in real time. Human sentence processing is incremental. As a person hears a sentence word by word, they build partial interpretations and make predictions about upcoming words. Garden path sentences temporarily mislead the listener. The old man the boats. Most listeners first read the old man as a noun phrase, but then reach the verb man and realize man is a verb meaning to staff, so the sentence means the old people staff the boats. Humans sometimes go down the wrong parse and then reanalyze. Speech production involves lexical access, retrieving words from memory. Humans sometimes experience tip of the tongue states where they know a word but cannot retrieve it. They might say it starts with a certain letter or give a definition while searching. Speech errors like spoonerisms, saying you have hissed all my mystery lectures instead of you have missed all my history lectures, reveal the underlying planning units in the brain. Understanding human cognition helps an AI model how people actually speak, which is often messy, incomplete, and full of errors. Psychology is the scientific study of mind and behavior. The major perspectives in psychology include behavioral, cognitive, psychodynamic, humanistic, biological, evolutionary, and sociocultural. The behavioral perspective, associated with Pavlov, Watson, and Skinner, focuses on observable behavior and how it is shaped by reinforcement and punishment. Classical conditioning pairs a neutral stimulus with an unconditioned stimulus to produce a conditioned response. Pavlov's dog learned to salivate at the sound of a bell because the bell was repeatedly paired with food. Operant conditioning changes behavior based on consequences. Reinforcement increases behavior, punishment decreases behavior. Positive reinforcement adds something desirable. Negative reinforcement removes something aversive. Positive punishment adds something aversive. Negative punishment removes something desirable. The cognitive perspective focuses on mental processes like memory, attention, perception, problem solving, and decision making. Jean Piaget studied cognitive development in children, identifying stages from sensorimotor to preoperational to concrete operational to formal operational. Lev Vygotsky emphasized social learning and the zone of proximal development, the gap between what a child can do alone and with help. The psychodynamic perspective, founded by Sigmund Freud, emphasizes unconscious drives, childhood experiences, and defense mechanisms. Freud proposed the id for primitive urges, the ego for reality based decision making, and the superego for internalized morals. Defense mechanisms like repression, projection, denial, rationalization, and sublimation protect the ego from anxiety. The humanistic perspective, associated with Carl Rogers and Abraham Maslow, emphasizes free will, self actualization, and the inherent drive to grow. Maslow's hierarchy of needs places physiological needs at the base, then safety, then belonging and love, then esteem, then self actualization at the peak. Rogers emphasized unconditional positive regard, empathy, and genuineness as necessary for healthy psychological development. The biological perspective examines the brain, nervous system, genetics, and neurochemistry. Neurons communicate across synapses using neurotransmitters like dopamine, serotonin, norepinephrine, GABA, and glutamate. Different brain regions have different functions. The amygdala processes fear and emotion. The hippocampus forms new memories. The prefrontal cortex handles executive functions like planning, impulse control, and decision making. The biological perspective also studies how hormones influence behavior and how genetics contribute to personality and mental disorders. The evolutionary perspective applies principles of natural selection to behavior. Fears of snakes and spiders are common because ancestors who avoided them survived longer. Altruism toward relatives can be explained by kin selection, helping copies of shared genes. Sexual selection explains differences in mate preferences between genders. The sociocultural perspective examines how social and cultural contexts shape behavior. Norms are unwritten rules for behavior in a group. Roles are expected behaviors for a particular position, like teacher or parent. Conformity is changing behavior to match the group, demonstrated in Asch's line experiments. Obedience is following orders from an authority figure, demonstrated in Milgram's shock experiments. Groupthink occurs when the desire for harmony overrides realistic decision making. Personality psychology studies individual differences in patterns of thinking, feeling, and behaving. The trait approach describes personality along continuous dimensions. The Big Five model, also called OCEAN, includes openness to experience, conscientiousness, extraversion, agreeableness, and neuroticism. Openness is curiosity and preference for novelty versus routine. Conscientiousness is organization, dependability, and self discipline versus carelessness. Extraversion is sociability, energy, and assertiveness versus introversion. Agreeableness is compassion and cooperation versus suspicion and hostility. Neuroticism is emotional instability and tendency toward negative emotions versus emotional stability. Each trait exists on a spectrum, and most people fall somewhere in the middle. The psychodynamic approach to personality emphasizes unconscious conflicts and defense mechanisms. The humanistic approach emphasizes self concept and the drive toward self actualization. The social cognitive approach, associated with Albert Bandura, emphasizes reciprocal determinism where behavior, personal factors, and environment all influence each other. Self efficacy is the belief in one's ability to succeed at a task. Locus of control, internal versus external, describes whether a person attributes outcomes to their own actions or to outside forces. The biological approach to personality includes temperament, which is the innate, biologically based emotional and behavioral style present from infancy. Thomas and Chess identified three temperament types: easy, difficult, and slow to warm up. Jerome Kagan studied behavioral inhibition, the tendency to be shy or bold in new situations. Social psychology studies how people think about, influence, and relate to others. Attribution theory explains how people assign causes to behavior. The fundamental attribution error is the tendency to overemphasize personality factors and underemphasize situational factors when explaining others' behavior. When explaining our own behavior, we show the actor observer bias, attributing our actions to the situation. Cognitive dissonance is the discomfort felt when holding two conflicting beliefs or when behavior contradicts beliefs. To reduce dissonance, people change their beliefs, change their behavior, or add new beliefs. The theory of planned behavior says that intentions are the best predictor of behavior, and intentions are shaped by attitudes, subjective norms, and perceived behavioral control. Stereotypes are generalized beliefs about groups of people. Prejudice is negative attitudes toward a group. Discrimination is negative behavior toward members of a group. The contact hypothesis states that under appropriate conditions, direct contact between groups can reduce prejudice. These conditions include equal status, common goals, intergroup cooperation, and support from authorities. The mere exposure effect says that repeated exposure to a stimulus increases liking for it. The halo effect is the tendency to let one positive trait, like physical attractiveness, influence overall impression. Bystander intervention is influenced by the number of people present. The more bystanders, the less likely any one person is to help, a phenomenon called the bystander effect or diffusion of responsibility. Developmental psychology studies how humans change across the lifespan. Prenatal development proceeds from zygote to embryo to fetus. Teratogens like alcohol and certain drugs can cause birth defects. Infants are born with reflexes like rooting, sucking, grasping, and Moro. Attachment theory, developed by John Bowlby and Mary Ainsworth, describes the emotional bond between infant and caregiver. Secure attachment occurs when caregivers are responsive, and securely attached infants explore freely and seek comfort from the caregiver when distressed. Insecure avoidant attachment occurs when caregivers are rejecting, and infants avoid the caregiver. Insecure anxious ambivalent attachment occurs when caregivers are inconsistent, and infants show clingy and then resistant behavior. Disorganized attachment occurs in frightening caregiving and is associated with later problems. Attachment style in childhood predicts relationship patterns in adulthood. Piaget's stages of cognitive development include sensorimotor from birth to two years, object permanence develops during this stage. Preoperational from two to seven years, characterized by egocentrism, inability to conserve, and symbolic thinking. Concrete operational from seven to eleven years, conservation and logical thinking about concrete objects emerges. Formal operational from eleven years onward, abstract and hypothetical thinking emerges. Kohlberg's stages of moral development include preconventional morality focused on punishment and reward, conventional morality focused on laws and social rules, and postconventional morality focused on universal ethical principles. Gilligan criticized Kohlberg for being male centric and proposed an ethic of care. Erikson's psychosocial stages describe eight conflicts from infancy to old age. Trust versus mistrust in infancy. Autonomy versus shame and doubt in toddlerhood. Initiative versus guilt in preschool age. Industry versus inferiority in school age. Identity versus role confusion in adolescence. Intimacy versus isolation in young adulthood. Generativity versus stagnation in middle adulthood. Integrity versus despair in late adulthood. Emotional psychology studies the nature and function of emotions. Emotions are brief, multifaceted responses to events that matter to the individual. They include subjective experience, physiological changes, cognitive appraisal, and behavioral expression. Basic emotions like happiness, sadness, fear, anger, surprise, and disgust are recognized across cultures. Complex emotions like shame, guilt, pride, and jealousy require self awareness and social comparison. The James Lange theory of emotion says that physiological arousal precedes and causes emotion. I feel afraid because I am trembling. The Cannon Bard theory says that physiological arousal and emotional experience occur simultaneously and independently. The Schachter Singer two factor theory says that emotion results from physiological arousal plus a cognitive label for that arousal based on context. The facial feedback hypothesis says that facial expressions influence emotional experience. Smiling can make you feel happier. Emotion regulation strategies include reappraisal, changing how you think about a situation, and suppression, inhibiting emotional expression. Reappraisal is generally more effective for well being. Motivation is the force that directs behavior toward goals. Intrinsic motivation comes from within, doing an activity for its own sake. Extrinsic motivation comes from external rewards or punishments. Overjustification effect occurs when extrinsic rewards reduce intrinsic motivation for an already enjoyable activity. Drive reduction theory says that physiological needs create drives that motivate behavior to reduce those drives, like eating to reduce hunger. Arousal theory says that people seek an optimal level of arousal, not too high and not too low. Yerkes Dodson law says that performance increases with arousal up to a point, then decreases with too high arousal. Self determination theory says that three basic psychological needs must be met for well being: autonomy, competence, and relatedness. Maslow's hierarchy places self actualization at the top after lower needs are met. Expectancy theory says that motivation depends on expecting that effort will lead to performance and performance will lead to desired outcomes. Goal setting theory says that specific, challenging, and attainable goals increase motivation and performance. Feedback on progress is also important. Abnormal psychology studies patterns of thought, emotion, and behavior that cause distress or impairment. The biopsychosocial model explains mental disorders as resulting from biological vulnerabilities, psychological factors, and social context. The DSM, Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, is the standard classification system used by clinicians. Anxiety disorders involve excessive fear or anxiety. Generalized anxiety disorder is chronic, excessive worry about many things. Panic disorder involves sudden, intense panic attacks. Social anxiety disorder is fear of social judgment. Specific phobias are intense fears of specific objects or situations. Obsessive compulsive disorder, OCD, involves obsessions, intrusive repetitive thoughts, and compulsions, repetitive behaviors performed to reduce anxiety from obsessions. Trauma and stressor related disorders include post traumatic stress disorder, PTSD, which occurs after exposure to a traumatic event and involves intrusive memories, avoidance, negative mood changes, and hyperarousal. Depressive disorders include major depressive disorder, characterized by persistent sadness, loss of interest, changes in sleep and appetite, fatigue, worthlessness, and thoughts of death. Bipolar disorder involves episodes of mania, elevated or irritable mood with increased energy and impulsivity, alternating with depressive episodes. Schizophrenia spectrum disorders involve psychosis, loss of contact with reality. Positive symptoms include hallucinations, false sensory perceptions like hearing voices, and delusions, fixed false beliefs. Negative symptoms include flat affect, reduced speech, and lack of motivation. Cognitive symptoms include problems with attention and memory. Personality disorders are enduring, inflexible patterns of behavior that cause distress or impairment. Cluster A includes paranoid, schizoid, and schizotypal, odd or eccentric. Cluster B includes antisocial, borderline, histrionic, and narcissistic, dramatic or erratic. Cluster C includes avoidant, dependent, and obsessive compulsive, anxious or fearful. Borderline personality disorder features instability in relationships, self image, and emotions, along with impulsive behavior and fear of abandonment. Antisocial personality disorder features disregard for others rights, lack of empathy, and impulsivity. Treatment approaches include psychotherapy and medication. Psychodynamic therapy explores unconscious conflicts and early experiences. Behavioral therapy uses learning principles to change maladaptive behaviors. Exposure therapy gradually exposes patients to feared stimuli to reduce avoidance. Cognitive therapy, developed by Aaron Beck, identifies and changes distorted thinking patterns. Cognitive behavioral therapy, CBT, combines cognitive and behavioral techniques and is one of the most effective treatments for many disorders. Humanistic therapy, including client centered therapy by Rogers, provides empathy, unconditional positive regard, and genuineness to help clients grow. Group therapy leverages social support and feedback from peers. Family therapy treats the family system rather than just the individual. Medications for mental disorders include antidepressants like SSRIs which increase serotonin levels, antianxiety medications like benzodiazepines, mood stabilizers like lithium for bipolar disorder, and antipsychotics for schizophrenia. The therapeutic alliance, the collaborative relationship between therapist and client, is one of the strongest predictors of positive outcomes regardless of therapy type. Human conversation is never purely informational. It always carries social and emotional subtext. People ask questions not just to get facts but to build relationships, test trust, express care, show off knowledge, seek validation, avoid silence, change topics, end conversations, or assert dominance. An AI that answers only the literal question without recognizing the social intent will feel robotic. For example, if a person asks what is the weather like today, the literal answer is a temperature and condition. But the person might really be asking should I bring a jacket, which requires a different answer. Or they might be making small talk to be polite, in which case a very detailed weather report is excessive. Matching the social function is key. People also use questions to respond to other questions. If someone asks where are you from, and you ask why do you want to know, that question responds with another question to probe intent before answering. This is called a counter question and is very human. People also answer questions with stories rather than direct facts. If asked about their childhood, they do not give a data sheet of birth date and school names. They tell an anecdote about a specific memory. Stories are how humans organize and share experience. An AI that gives bullet point facts instead of narrative will feel unnatural. Turn taking in conversation is finely coordinated. Humans avoid overlapping speech except in moments of excitement or interruption, and they avoid long silences. The average gap between turns is about two hundred milliseconds. Longer gaps indicate disagreement, confusion, or reluctance. Shorter gaps indicate enthusiasm or intimacy. Gaze, facial expression, and tone of voice provide additional layers of meaning. A question asked with a rising intonation at the end is a genuine question. The same words said with flat or falling intonation might be a rhetorical question or a command. Humans use prosody to mark sarcasm, sincerity, doubt, excitement, boredom, and anger. An AI limited to text cannot use tone, but it can use word choice and punctuation to signal intent. Exclamation marks signal enthusiasm or urgency. Ellipses like this signal hesitation or uncertainty. Emojis, while not formal language, have become standard for conveying tone in text based conversation. A period at the end of a short message can seem cold or abrupt. Leaving off the period seems more casual and friendly. These are the pragmatics of text messaging. Natural language understanding requires world knowledge and common sense reasoning. If someone says I dropped my sandwich and then my dog ate it, humans know that the dog ate the sandwich, not the act of dropping. If someone says the city council refused the protesters a permit because they advocated violence, humans know that they refers to the protesters, not the city council, based on semantic role and real world knowledge about who advocates violence. If someone says the trophy would not fit into the brown suitcase because it was too big, humans know it was too big refers to the trophy, not the suitcase, because trophies are typically larger than suitcases. This kind of common sense is trivial for humans but extremely difficult for AI. Humans also understand scalar implicature. If someone says some of the students passed the exam, the listener infers that not all passed, because if all had passed, the speaker would have said all. If someone says the food was good, the listener infers it was not excellent, because excellent would have been said if true. These inferences are automatic and not part of the literal meaning. Another key aspect of human conversation is perspective taking or theory of mind. Theory of mind is the ability to attribute mental states, beliefs, intentions, desires, and knowledge to oneself and others. It allows a speaker to know what the listener already knows and what they need to be told. It allows a listener to infer what the speaker intended even if the words are ambiguous. A child who lacks theory of mind, like a child with autism, may talk at length about their own interest without realizing the listener is bored or already knows the information. An AI needs a model of the user's knowledge to answer appropriately. If the user asks a basic question, the AI should not respond with a graduate level lecture. If the user uses technical jargon, the AI can assume shared expertise. Adjusting complexity to the listener is a fundamental human skill. Memory for conversation is also important. Humans refer back to earlier parts of a conversation. They use definite references like that thing we talked about earlier. They remember what the other person said and use it later to show listening. They notice if the other person contradicts themselves. An AI that treats each question as independent and forgets the previous exchange will feel disjointed and impersonal. Conversational memory of at least a few turns is necessary for coherence. Humans also use discourse markers to structure conversation. Words like so, well, now, then, anyway, actually, basically, honestly, and I mean signal how a statement relates to previous statements. So can introduce a conclusion or consequence. Well can signal hesitation or disagreement. Actually can correct a previous statement politely. Anyway can return to a previous topic after a digression. These markers are almost meaningless semantically but are crucial for flow. Politeness in language is highly cultural. In some cultures, directness is valued as honest. In others, indirectness is valued as respectful. The same question phrased as give me the salt might be acceptable in some contexts but rude in others. Could you please pass the salt might be appropriate in formal settings. Salt, please might be fine among friends. Understanding register, the level of formality appropriate to the situation, is essential. Using overly formal language in a casual setting sounds stiff. Using slang in a formal setting sounds unprofessional. Humans code switch, shifting between registers, depending on who they are talking to and where. The study of human behavior and personality also includes the concept of self. The self is the sense of who one is, including self concept, the cognitive representation of one's traits, and self esteem, the evaluative feeling about oneself. Self awareness is the ability to reflect on oneself as an object of attention. The looking glass self theory says that we develop our self concept by imagining how others see us. Social comparison theory says that we evaluate ourselves by comparing to others, upward comparison to those better off and downward comparison to those worse off. Self serving bias is the tendency to attribute successes to internal factors and failures to external factors. The fundamental need for belonging drives much of human behavior. Loneliness is a painful signal that social connections are insufficient. Ostracism, being excluded by others, activates brain regions associated with physical pain. People go to great lengths to gain social acceptance and avoid rejection. Conformity, obedience, and compliance are forms of social influence that stem from this need. Persuasion can follow two routes according to the elaboration likelihood model. The central route involves careful thinking about arguments and is more enduring. The peripheral route involves cues like attractiveness or authority and is more superficial. Emotions influence reasoning far more than classical models of rationality admit. Fear makes people more risk averse and more likely to seek certainty. Anger makes people more optimistic about their own chances and more likely to blame individuals rather than situations. Sadness makes people more willing to pay to change their circumstances. Happiness makes people more creative but also more prone to cognitive biases. Understanding emotion is essential for predicting human responses. Personality consistency is not absolute. People behave differently across situations, but they also have stable patterns. Situation strength theory says that strong situations, like a job interview, constrain behavior and reduce personality expression, while weak situations, like a party, allow personality to emerge. The person situation debate has been resolved by recognizing that both matter. Behavior is a function of personality and situation, along with their interaction. Personality development continues across the lifespan. Traits show increasing stability with age. Conscientiousness and agreeableness tend to increase from young adulthood to middle age. Neuroticism tends to decline. Openness tends to decline slightly in older age. Extraversion tends to decline, but sociability as distinct from energy may remain stable. Life events like marriage, parenthood, and career changes can produce personality change, but people also select into environments that fit their existing traits. The concept of psychological well being includes not just happiness but meaning, purpose, mastery, autonomy, personal growth, positive relationships, and self acceptance. Subjective well being is an individual's self reported evaluation of their life satisfaction and emotional balance. Hedonic adaptation is the tendency to return to a baseline level of happiness after positive or negative events. The set point theory of happiness suggests that about half of the variance in well being is heritable, but intentional activities and life circumstances also matter. The pursuit of happiness is not always straightforward. Trying too hard to be happy can backfire. Valuing happiness highly is paradoxically associated with lower well being because people become disappointed when they are not happy. Acceptance of negative emotions, rather than suppression, is associated with better mental health. Mindfulness, the nonjudgmental awareness of the present moment, has been shown to reduce stress, anxiety, and depression. Mindfulness practices train attention regulation, body awareness, and emotion regulation. The default mode network of the brain is active when people are not focused on external tasks, associated with mind wandering, self referential thought, and rumination. Meditation reduces default mode network activity, which correlates with reduced mind wandering and increased present focus. All of this knowledge about language, conversation, psychology, and personality must be integrated for an AI to talk like a human. The AI must predict not just the next word but the next social move. It must recognize when a question is genuine or rhetorical, when an answer is expected or optional, when to be direct or indirect, when to be brief or elaborate, when to joke or be serious, when to agree or disagree, when to ask a clarifying question or answer confidently. It must track the user's knowledge state, emotional state, and conversational goals. It must maintain coherence across multiple turns. It must signal attention, understanding, empathy, and appropriate emotion through word choice, sentence length, punctuation, and pacing. It must recover gracefully from errors. It must learn from each interaction. This is the challenge of humanlike AI.
