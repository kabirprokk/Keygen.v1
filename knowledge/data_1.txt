Classical mechanics is the branch of physics that deals with the motion of macroscopic objects under the influence of forces. It is rooted in the works of Sir Isaac Newton, who formulated the three laws of motion in 1687 with his publication of Philosophiæ Naturalis Principia Mathematica. Newton's first law states that an object at rest stays at rest and an object in motion continues in motion with the same speed and in the same direction unless acted upon by an unbalanced external force. This property of matter to resist changes in its state of motion is called inertia. Newton's second law defines the relationship between force, mass, and acceleration, expressed mathematically as F equals m times a, where F is the net force applied to an object, m is its mass, and a is the acceleration produced. Newton's third law asserts that for every action there is an equal and opposite reaction. This means that if object A exerts a force on object B, then object B simultaneously exerts a force of equal magnitude but opposite direction on object A. Classical mechanics also includes the concepts of work, energy, and power. Work is defined as the product of force and displacement in the direction of the force. Kinetic energy is the energy of motion and is given by one half times mass times velocity squared. Potential energy is stored energy due to an object's position or configuration, such as gravitational potential energy which equals mass times gravitational acceleration times height. The law of conservation of energy states that the total energy in an isolated system remains constant over time, although it can transform from kinetic to potential and vice versa. Momentum is another fundamental conserved quantity in classical mechanics. Linear momentum is the product of mass and velocity, and the total momentum of a closed system is conserved if no external forces act upon it. This principle is extremely useful for analyzing collisions and explosions. Angular momentum is the rotational analog of linear momentum and is conserved in systems where no external torque is applied. Rotational motion is described using angular velocity, angular acceleration, and moment of inertia, which plays the same role for rotation that mass plays for linear motion. The moment of inertia depends on the distribution of mass relative to the axis of rotation. Torque is the rotational equivalent of force and is calculated as the product of force and lever arm distance. The equations of rotational motion directly mirror those of linear motion, with angular displacement replacing linear displacement, angular velocity replacing linear velocity, and angular acceleration replacing linear acceleration. Classical mechanics also includes the study of oscillatory motion, such as springs and pendulums. Hooke's law states that the force exerted by a spring is proportional to the displacement from its equilibrium position and acts in the opposite direction, expressed as F equals negative k times x, where k is the spring constant and x is displacement. Simple harmonic motion occurs when the restoring force is directly proportional to displacement, leading to sinusoidal oscillations with a characteristic frequency determined by the mass and spring constant. A simple pendulum also undergoes approximately simple harmonic motion for small angular displacements, with its period depending only on the length of the pendulum and the acceleration due to gravity. Damped oscillations occur when a frictional force, such as air resistance or internal friction, dissipates energy from the system, causing the amplitude to decrease exponentially over time. Driven oscillations occur when an external periodic force is applied to the system, and if the driving frequency matches the natural frequency of the system, resonance occurs, leading to a dramatic increase in amplitude. The study of classical mechanics also extends to the motion of rigid bodies, which are objects that do not deform under applied forces. Rigid body motion can be decomposed into translation of the center of mass and rotation about the center of mass. The center of mass of a system is the point where the total mass of the system can be considered to be concentrated for the purpose of analyzing translational motion. For a system of particles, the center of mass is the weighted average of their positions based on their masses. The motion of the center of mass is determined solely by the net external force acting on the system, regardless of the internal forces. This principle simplifies the analysis of complex systems such as projectiles, vehicles, and celestial bodies. Classical mechanics also includes the concept of gravitational force as described by Newton's law of universal gravitation. This law states that every particle in the universe attracts every other particle with a force that is directly proportional to the product of their masses and inversely proportional to the square of the distance between their centers. The gravitational constant G, which has a value of approximately 6.674 times ten to the negative eleventh power cubic meters per kilogram per second squared, quantifies the strength of this force. Newton's law of gravitation successfully explains the orbits of planets around the sun, the motion of moons around planets, and the tides on Earth caused by the moon and sun. Kepler's laws of planetary motion, which were derived empirically from astronomical observations, can be derived from Newton's law of gravitation and his laws of motion. Kepler's first law states that planets move in elliptical orbits with the sun at one focus. Kepler's second law states that a line segment joining a planet and the sun sweeps out equal areas during equal intervals of time, which is a consequence of conservation of angular momentum. Kepler's third law states that the square of the orbital period of a planet is proportional to the cube of the semi-major axis of its orbit. While classical mechanics was tremendously successful, it has limitations. It fails to accurately describe phenomena at very high speeds close to the speed of light, where special relativity is required, and at very small scales, such as the size of atoms, where quantum mechanics is required. Nevertheless, for most everyday situations involving objects larger than molecules and speeds much slower than light, classical mechanics remains an accurate and indispensable tool.

Thermodynamics is the branch of physics that deals with heat, work, temperature, and the relationships between them. It describes how thermal energy is converted to and from other forms of energy and how it affects matter. The foundation of thermodynamics rests upon four fundamental laws. The zeroth law of thermodynamics establishes the concept of temperature. It states that if two systems are each in thermal equilibrium with a third system, then they are in thermal equilibrium with each other. This law allows the use of thermometers to measure temperature by ensuring that the thermometer reaches thermal equilibrium with the object being measured. The first law of thermodynamics is essentially the law of conservation of energy applied to thermodynamic systems. It states that the change in internal energy of a system is equal to the heat added to the system minus the work done by the system. Mathematically, this is expressed as delta U equals Q minus W, where delta U is the change in internal energy, Q is the heat transferred into the system, and W is the work done by the system. Internal energy is the total energy stored within a system, including the kinetic and potential energies of its molecules. Heat is the transfer of thermal energy due to a temperature difference, while work is the transfer of energy via macroscopic forces. The first law implies that energy cannot be created or destroyed, only transformed from one form to another. Perpetual motion machines of the first kind, which would create energy from nothing, are therefore impossible. The second law of thermodynamics introduces the concept of entropy and imposes directionality on thermodynamic processes. It states that the total entropy of an isolated system can never decrease over time. Entropy is a measure of the disorder or randomness of a system. In any natural process, the entropy of the universe either increases or, in the idealized case of a reversible process, remains constant. This means that heat cannot spontaneously flow from a colder body to a hotter body without external work being done. The second law also implies that no heat engine can be one hundred percent efficient, because some heat must always be rejected to a cold reservoir. The maximum possible efficiency of a heat engine operating between two temperatures is given by the Carnot efficiency, which is one minus the cold reservoir temperature divided by the hot reservoir temperature, where temperatures are measured in kelvins. The second law also prohibits perpetual motion machines of the second kind, which would extract heat from a single reservoir and convert it entirely into useful work. The third law of thermodynamics states that as the temperature of a system approaches absolute zero, the entropy of the system approaches a constant minimum. Absolute zero is zero kelvins, which corresponds to negative two hundred seventy three point fifteen degrees Celsius. At absolute zero, the system would be in its ground state, the state of lowest possible energy. However, it is impossible to reach absolute zero through any finite number of processes. The third law provides a reference point for measuring absolute entropy values. Thermodynamics also deals with various thermodynamic processes, such as isothermal, adiabatic, isobaric, and isochoric processes. An isothermal process occurs at constant temperature. For an ideal gas undergoing isothermal expansion, the internal energy remains constant, so all heat added is converted into work. An adiabatic process occurs without any heat transfer between the system and its surroundings. During adiabatic expansion, a gas does work on its surroundings, causing its internal energy and temperature to decrease. Conversely, adiabatic compression increases the temperature of the gas. An isobaric process occurs at constant pressure. The work done during isobaric expansion is simply pressure times the change in volume. An isochoric process occurs at constant volume. Since no work is done during an isochoric process, any heat added goes entirely into increasing the internal energy and thus the temperature of the system. Thermodynamic systems can be open, closed, or isolated. An open system can exchange both matter and energy with its surroundings. A closed system can exchange energy but not matter. An isolated system can exchange neither matter nor energy. The behavior of gases is often approximated using the ideal gas law, which states that pressure times volume equals number of moles times the universal gas constant times temperature. Real gases deviate from this ideal behavior at high pressures and low temperatures, where intermolecular forces and the finite volume of gas molecules become significant. The van der Waals equation is an equation of state that accounts for these deviations by introducing correction terms for molecular volume and attractive forces. Thermodynamics also covers phase changes, such as melting, freezing, vaporization, condensation, and sublimation. Latent heat is the heat absorbed or released during a phase change at constant temperature. For example, the latent heat of fusion is the heat required to change a substance from solid to liquid, while the latent heat of vaporization is the heat required to change from liquid to gas. The Clausius-Clapeyron equation describes the relationship between pressure and temperature along a phase boundary. Thermodynamics has numerous applications, including the design of engines, refrigerators, heat pumps, power plants, and chemical reactors. It is also fundamental to understanding atmospheric science, astrophysics, biology, and materials science.

Electromagnetism is the branch of physics that deals with the electromagnetic force, which is one of the four fundamental forces of nature. The electromagnetic force acts between electrically charged particles and is responsible for a vast array of phenomena, including electricity, magnetism, light, and other electromagnetic waves. The study of electromagnetism began with observations of static electricity and magnetism in ancient times, but it was systematically developed in the nineteenth century by scientists such as Hans Christian Ørsted, André-Marie Ampère, Michael Faraday, and James Clerk Maxwell. Ørsted discovered that an electric current flowing through a wire could deflect a compass needle, demonstrating a direct relationship between electricity and magnetism. Ampère quantified this relationship and formulated Ampère's law, which states that the magnetic field produced by a steady current is proportional to the current and depends on the geometry of the conductor. Faraday discovered electromagnetic induction, which is the generation of an electromotive force across a conductor when it is exposed to a changing magnetic field. Faraday's law of induction states that the induced electromotive force is equal to the negative rate of change of magnetic flux through a circuit. This principle is the basis for electric generators and transformers. Lenz's law provides the direction of the induced current: it always opposes the change in magnetic flux that produced it. The entire theory of classical electromagnetism is summarized by Maxwell's equations, a set of four partial differential equations that describe how electric and magnetic fields are generated and altered by charges and currents. Gauss's law for electricity states that the electric flux through any closed surface is proportional to the enclosed electric charge. This means that electric field lines originate on positive charges and terminate on negative charges. Gauss's law for magnetism states that the magnetic flux through any closed surface is zero. This implies that there are no magnetic monopoles; magnetic field lines always form closed loops. Faraday's law of induction, already mentioned, relates a changing magnetic field to an induced electric field. Ampère's law, as modified by Maxwell to include the displacement current, states that magnetic fields can be generated by electric currents and by changing electric fields. The addition of the displacement current term was a key insight that allowed Maxwell to predict the existence of electromagnetic waves. Electromagnetic waves are self-propagating oscillations of electric and magnetic fields that travel through space at the speed of light. Maxwell showed that the speed of electromagnetic waves in a vacuum is determined by the permittivity and permeability of free space, and this speed matched the known speed of light, leading Maxwell to conclude that light itself is an electromagnetic wave. The electromagnetic spectrum encompasses all possible frequencies of electromagnetic radiation. From lowest frequency to highest frequency, the spectrum includes radio waves, microwaves, terahertz radiation, infrared radiation, visible light, ultraviolet radiation, X-rays, and gamma rays. Radio waves have the longest wavelengths and lowest frequencies, and they are used for communication, radar, and radio astronomy. Microwaves have shorter wavelengths and are used for wireless communication, microwave ovens, and radar. Infrared radiation is felt as heat and is used in thermal imaging, remote controls, and spectroscopy. Visible light is the narrow band of frequencies that can be detected by the human eye, ranging from red the longest wavelength to violet the shortest wavelength. Ultraviolet radiation has higher frequencies than visible light and can cause sunburn and is used in sterilization and fluorescence. X-rays have even higher frequencies and are used in medical imaging and crystallography. Gamma rays have the highest frequencies and shortest wavelengths and are produced by nuclear reactions and radioactive decay; they are used in cancer treatment and astrophysics. The electric field is a vector field that surrounds electric charges and exerts force on other charges. The electric field at a point is defined as the force per unit positive test charge placed at that point. For a point charge, the electric field radiates radially outward if the charge is positive and inward if the charge is negative. The electric field obeys the principle of superposition, meaning that the total electric field from multiple charges is the vector sum of the fields from each individual charge. Electric potential is the potential energy per unit charge associated with the electric field. Voltage is the difference in electric potential between two points. A charge moving in an electric field experiences a force and, if free to move, will accelerate, gaining kinetic energy. The magnetic field is a vector field that exerts force on moving charges and on current-carrying wires. The magnetic force on a moving charge is perpendicular to both the velocity of the charge and the magnetic field, and its magnitude is given by the charge times the velocity times the magnetic field strength times the sine of the angle between velocity and field. This force is responsible for the circular or helical motion of charged particles in magnetic fields, as observed in cyclotrons and mass spectrometers. The magnetic field due to a long straight wire is circular around the wire, with the direction given by the right-hand rule. The magnetic field inside a solenoid, a coil of wire, is nearly uniform and parallel to the axis of the coil, and its strength is proportional to the current and the number of turns per unit length. Electromagnetic induction is the phenomenon where a changing magnetic field produces an electric field. This is the operating principle of electric generators, where mechanical energy is used to rotate a coil in a magnetic field, producing an alternating current. Transformers use mutual induction to change voltage levels in alternating current circuits. A transformer consists of two coils wound around a common iron core. An alternating current in the primary coil produces a changing magnetic flux in the core, which induces an alternating voltage in the secondary coil. The ratio of the voltages equals the ratio of the number of turns in the coils. The Lorentz force law combines the electric and magnetic forces on a charged particle. The total force on a charge is the sum of the electric force and the magnetic force. This law is fundamental to the operation of many devices, including cathode ray tubes, particle accelerators, and mass spectrometers. In materials, electromagnetism explains a wide range of properties. Conductors are materials that allow electric charges to flow freely, typically because they have free electrons. Metals are good conductors. Insulators, such as rubber or glass, have tightly bound electrons that do not flow easily. Semiconductors, such as silicon and germanium, have electrical conductivity between that of conductors and insulators and are the basis of modern electronics. Dielectric materials are insulators that can be polarized by an electric field, meaning that their molecules align with the field, reducing the overall field inside the material. Magnetic materials can be classified based on their response to magnetic fields. Diamagnetic materials create an induced magnetic field in opposition to an externally applied field and are weakly repelled by magnets. Paramagnetic materials have unpaired electrons that align with an external field, causing a weak attraction. Ferromagnetic materials, such as iron, nickel, and cobalt, exhibit strong, permanent magnetism because their magnetic domains align even in the absence of an external field. Above the Curie temperature, ferromagnetic materials lose their permanent magnetism and become paramagnetic. Electromagnetism is also essential to understanding optics. When an electromagnetic wave encounters a boundary between two media, part of the wave is reflected and part is transmitted or refracted. Refraction is the bending of light as it passes from one medium to another due to a change in speed. Snell's law relates the angles of incidence and refraction to the refractive indices of the two media. Dispersion is the phenomenon where the refractive index depends on frequency, causing white light to separate into its constituent colors when passing through a prism. Diffraction is the bending of waves around obstacles or through openings, and interference is the superposition of waves leading to constructive or destructive interference. Young's double-slit experiment provided strong evidence for the wave nature of light by demonstrating interference patterns. Polarization is the orientation of the electric field oscillations of an electromagnetic wave. Light can be linearly, circularly, or elliptically polarized. Some materials, such as polarizing filters, only transmit light with a specific polarization. The photoelectric effect, in which light incident on a metal surface ejects electrons, could not be explained by classical electromagnetism and led to the development of quantum mechanics. In the photoelectric effect, Einstein proposed that light consists of discrete packets of energy called photons, each with energy proportional to its frequency. This particle-like behavior of light, combined with its wave-like behavior observed in interference and diffraction, illustrates wave-particle duality, a central concept in modern physics. Despite this limitation, classical electromagnetism remains an extraordinarily successful theory for describing macroscopic electrical and magnetic phenomena, and it forms the foundation for electrical engineering, optics, radio technology, and many other fields.

Statistical mechanics is a bridge between the microscopic world of atoms and molecules and the macroscopic world of thermodynamics. It provides a framework for explaining the laws of thermodynamics based on the statistical behavior of large ensembles of particles. The key concept in statistical mechanics is the partition function, which encodes all the thermodynamic information about a system. The partition function is a sum over all possible quantum states of the system of the Boltzmann factor, which is e to the power of negative energy divided by k times T, where k is Boltzmann's constant and T is temperature. From the partition function, one can derive thermodynamic quantities such as internal energy, entropy, free energy, and pressure. The Boltzmann distribution gives the probability that a system is in a particular state as a function of that state's energy and the temperature. At higher temperatures, higher energy states become more probable. The Maxwell-Boltzmann distribution describes the distribution of speeds of particles in an ideal gas. It shows that at a given temperature, some particles move slowly, some move very fast, but most have speeds near the most probable speed. The average kinetic energy of particles in an ideal gas is directly proportional to the absolute temperature. The equipartition theorem states that each degree of freedom of a molecule that contributes quadratically to its energy has an average energy of one half k T. For a monatomic ideal gas, there are three translational degrees of freedom, so the average energy per particle is three halves k T, and the total internal energy is three halves N k T. For diatomic molecules, rotational and vibrational degrees of freedom also contribute, although at ordinary temperatures, vibrational modes are often not fully excited due to quantum mechanical effects. Statistical mechanics also explains the concept of entropy as a measure of the number of microscopic configurations corresponding to a given macroscopic state. The Boltzmann entropy formula states that entropy equals k times the logarithm of the number of microstates. This statistical definition of entropy provides a natural explanation for the second law of thermodynamics: an isolated system evolves from a less probable state to a more probable state because the number of microstates increases. Fluctuations away from equilibrium become extremely rare for large systems, which is why the second law appears to be deterministic for macroscopic objects but can be violated on very small scales or short times. Statistical mechanics is essential for understanding phase transitions, such as the transition between liquid and gas. The Ising model, which consists of a lattice of spins that can be up or down, is a simple model that exhibits a phase transition from ordered to disordered states at a critical temperature. The model captures the essential physics of ferromagnetism and has been applied to many other systems. The theory of critical phenomena describes the behavior of systems near phase transitions, where properties such as heat capacity and susceptibility diverge following power laws with universal exponents. Statistical mechanics also underlies the kinetic theory of gases, which derives macroscopic properties like pressure, viscosity, thermal conductivity, and diffusion from the motions and collisions of molecules. The mean free path is the average distance a molecule travels between collisions. Transport phenomena, such as heat conduction and viscosity, arise from the transfer of energy, momentum, and mass due to molecular motion. The Boltzmann transport equation describes the evolution of the distribution function for particles in a gas under the influence of forces and collisions. Statistical mechanics has been extended to quantum systems, leading to quantum statistical mechanics. For particles obeying the Pauli exclusion principle, such as electrons, protons, and neutrons, the Fermi-Dirac statistics apply. Fermions cannot occupy the same quantum state simultaneously. At low temperatures, fermions fill up energy states from the lowest up to the Fermi energy, and this degenerate Fermi gas explains the properties of metals, white dwarf stars, and neutron stars. For particles that do not obey the exclusion principle, such as photons and certain atoms at low temperatures, Bose-Einstein statistics apply. Bosons can occupy the same quantum state, leading to phenomena such as Bose-Einstein condensation, where a macroscopic fraction of particles collapses into the lowest energy state at very low temperatures. Photon gas, which obeys Bose-Einstein statistics with zero chemical potential, explains blackbody radiation. Planck's law for blackbody radiation, which describes the spectral distribution of electromagnetic radiation from a perfect absorber and emitter, was one of the first successes of quantum statistical mechanics. The study of fluctuations, noise, and nonequilibrium statistical mechanics continues to be an active area of research, with applications in biology, finance, and complex systems. The fluctuation-dissipation theorem relates the response of a system to external perturbations to its spontaneous fluctuations at equilibrium. This theorem is fundamental to understanding Brownian motion, electrical noise in resistors, and many other phenomena. Overall, statistical mechanics provides a profound connection between the microscopic and macroscopic worlds, revealing how deterministic laws at the atomic scale give rise to the probabilistic but highly predictable behavior of bulk matter.
