The history of computing is a story of human ingenuity spanning thousands of years, beginning with simple counting devices and culminating in the modern digital computer. The earliest known computing device is the abacus, which originated in ancient Mesopotamia around 2500 BCE. The abacus allowed users to perform arithmetic operations by sliding beads along rods, and it remained the primary calculation tool for merchants and accountants for millennia. In the 17th century, Blaise Pascal invented the Pascaline, a mechanical calculator that could add and subtract using a system of gears and wheels. Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz later improved upon this design with the Stepped Reckoner, which could also multiply and divide. Leibniz also envisioned a universal language of reasoning and binary arithmetic, laying philosophical groundwork for future computing. The Industrial Revolution brought the need for more sophisticated calculation, particularly for producing mathematical tables used in navigation and engineering. Charles Babbage, an English mathematician and inventor, designed the Difference Engine in the 1820s to automatically compute polynomial functions and print accurate tables. Although the Difference Engine was never fully completed during Babbage's lifetime due to funding and manufacturing limitations, it is considered the first automatic computing machine. Babbage then designed the Analytical Engine, a far more ambitious device that included many features of modern computers: an arithmetic logic unit called the mill, memory called the store, conditional branching, and loops. The Analytical Engine was programmable using punched cards, an idea borrowed from the Jacquard loom which used cards to weave complex patterns in silk. Ada Lovelace, a mathematician and collaborator of Babbage, is often credited as the first computer programmer. She wrote detailed notes on the Analytical Engine, including an algorithm for computing Bernoulli numbers, which is recognized as the first computer program. Lovelace also speculated that such a machine could manipulate symbols beyond numbers, potentially composing music or performing other creative tasks. In the late 19th century, Herman Hollerith developed punched card tabulating machines to process data for the 1890 United States Census. His machines dramatically reduced the time needed to complete the census, and Hollerith's company eventually became part of International Business Machines, or IBM. The early 20th century saw advances in electromechanical computing, including the development of analog computers that represented quantities as continuous voltages or shaft rotations. Vannevar Bush built the Differential Analyzer at MIT in the 1930s, which was used to solve differential equations for engineering problems. The theoretical foundations of modern computing were established in the 1930s by Alan Turing, Alonzo Church, Kurt Gödel, and others. Alan Turing introduced the concept of a Turing machine, a simple abstract device that could perform any computable function given enough time and tape. The Turing machine formalized the notion of algorithm and computation, and Turing's work on the Entscheidungsproblem, or decision problem, led to the discovery that some problems are uncomputable. Turing also developed the concept of universal Turing machines, which can simulate any other Turing machine, providing the theoretical basis for stored-program computers. During World War II, the need for rapid codebreaking and ballistic calculations accelerated computer development. Alan Turing worked at Bletchley Park in Britain to crack the German Enigma cipher. The electromechanical Bombe, designed by Turing and others, helped decrypt German messages, significantly aiding the Allied war effort. The Colossus, designed by Tommy Flowers, was the world's first programmable electronic digital computer. Colossus used vacuum tubes to process data at high speed and was used to break the Lorenz cipher used by German high command. Because of wartime secrecy, the existence of Colossus remained unknown for decades. In the United States, the Electronic Numerical Integrator and Computer, or ENIAC, was developed at the University of Pennsylvania by John Presper Eckert and John Mauchly. Completed in 1945, ENIAC was the first general-purpose electronic digital computer. It contained nearly eighteen thousand vacuum tubes, occupied over one thousand square feet, and weighed thirty tons. Programming ENIAC required physically rewiring cables and setting switches, a task performed by a team of female mathematicians including Jean Bartik and Kathleen Antonelli. The stored-program concept, where both instructions and data reside in the same memory, was first implemented in the Small-Scale Experimental Machine, also known as the Manchester Baby, which ran its first program in 1948. The Manchester Mark I followed shortly after. The Electronic Delay Storage Automatic Calculator, or EDSAC, built at Cambridge University by Maurice Wilkes, was the first practical stored-program computer. John von Neumann, a mathematician who consulted on ENIAC, wrote a influential report describing the stored-program architecture, which became known as the von Neumann architecture. Most computers today still follow this basic design: a central processing unit, memory, input and output devices, and a bus connecting them. The 1950s saw the commercialization of computers. The UNIVAC I, built by Eckert and Mauchly, was the first commercially successful computer. It was used by the Census Bureau and famously predicted the outcome of the 1952 presidential election. IBM dominated the business computing market with the IBM 700 series and later the IBM 1401. These computers used magnetic tape and punched cards for storage, and they were programmed in assembly language or early high-level languages. The invention of the transistor at Bell Labs in 1947 by John Bardeen, Walter Brattain, and William Shockley revolutionized computing. Transistors were smaller, more reliable, consumed less power, and generated less heat than vacuum tubes. The first transistorized computer was the Manchester Transistor Computer in 1953. By the late 1950s, companies like IBM and Digital Equipment Corporation were building fully transistorized machines. The development of integrated circuits in the late 1950s and early 1960s, independently invented by Jack Kilby at Texas Instruments and Robert Noyce at Fairchild Semiconductor, allowed multiple transistors to be fabricated on a single silicon chip. Integrated circuits dramatically reduced the size and cost of computers. The IBM System 360, announced in 1964, was a family of compatible computers that used the same instruction set architecture across models, allowing customers to upgrade without rewriting software. The System 360 was one of the most successful products in computing history and established IBM as the dominant force in mainframe computing. The 1970s brought the microprocessor, a complete central processing unit on a single chip. The Intel 4004, released in 1971, was the first commercial microprocessor. It had two thousand three hundred transistors and operated at 740 kilohertz. The Intel 8008 and 8080 followed, enabling the development of personal computers. The Altair 8800, released in 1975 as a build-it-yourself kit, is often considered the first personal computer. It had no keyboard or display, only switches and blinking lights. Two young programmers, Bill Gates and Paul Allen, wrote a BASIC interpreter for the Altair, leading to the founding of Microsoft. The Apple II, released in 1977 by Steve Jobs and Steve Wozniak, was one of the first highly successful mass-produced personal computers. It featured color graphics, a keyboard, and a floppy disk drive. The IBM Personal Computer, or IBM PC, launched in 1981, used an Intel 8088 processor and Microsoft's MS-DOS operating system. The open architecture of the IBM PC allowed third-party manufacturers to produce compatible hardware and software, creating a vast ecosystem. Clone manufacturers like Compaq produced legal reverse-engineered versions of the IBM PC, driving down prices and expanding the market. The graphical user interface was pioneered at Xerox's Palo Alto Research Center, or PARC, in the 1970s. The Xerox Alto featured windows, icons, menus, a pointing device called a mouse, and Ethernet networking. While the Alto was never commercially successful, its ideas influenced the Apple Lisa, released in 1983, and the Apple Macintosh, released in 1984. The Macintosh popularized the graphical user interface and made computing accessible to non-technical users. Microsoft released Windows 1.0 in 1985, and later versions, particularly Windows 3.0 in 1990 and Windows 95, brought graphical interfaces to the majority of personal computers. The development of programming languages is closely intertwined with the history of computing. Machine language, consisting of binary codes directly executed by the processor, was the only way to program early computers. Assembly language replaced binary codes with human-readable mnemonics like ADD, SUB, and JMP, but each instruction still corresponded directly to one machine instruction. The first high-level programming language was Plankalkül, designed by Konrad Zuse in the 1940s, but it was never implemented. Short Code, developed in 1950, was one of the first practical high-level languages but required manual translation. Fortran, developed by IBM under the direction of John Backus, was released in 1957. Fortran was designed for scientific and engineering calculations and introduced many concepts still in use today, including variables, loops, conditionals, and subroutines. Fortran allowed programmers to write mathematical expressions in a notation similar to algebraic formulas, which were then compiled into efficient machine code. The success of Fortran proved that high-level languages could produce efficient programs while greatly improving programmer productivity. Lisp was developed by John McCarthy at MIT in 1958. Lisp was designed for artificial intelligence research and introduced the concept of symbolic computation, recursion as a primary control structure, and automatic garbage collection. Lisp is based on the lambda calculus and treats programs as data, a property known as homoiconicity. Many important AI systems were written in Lisp, and the language continues to be used in research and certain commercial applications. Algol, developed in the late 1950s and early 1960s by an international committee, was designed as a universal algorithmic language. Algol introduced block structure with begin and end delimiters, lexical scoping, and nested functions. While Algol itself was not widely adopted commercially, it influenced nearly every subsequent language, including Pascal, C, and Java. Cobol, or Common Business Oriented Language, was developed in 1959 by a committee led by Grace Hopper. Cobol was designed for business data processing, with English-like syntax and strong support for file handling and record structures. Cobol became the dominant language for mainframe business applications and is still used today in legacy banking, insurance, and government systems. Grace Hopper also developed the first compiler, which translated English-like instructions into machine code, and she popularized the term compiler. Basic, or Beginner's All-purpose Symbolic Instruction Code, was developed by John Kemeny and Thomas Kurtz at Dartmouth College in 1964. Basic was designed to be easy to learn for students with no programming experience, and it became the standard language for time-sharing systems and early personal computers. Microsoft's version of Basic brought programming to millions of hobbyists. Pascal, designed by Niklaus Wirth in 1970, was intended for teaching structured programming. Pascal introduced strong typing and structured control flow, and it was widely used in university computer science courses. The Apple Lisa and Macintosh software development kits included Pascal as a primary language. C was developed by Dennis Ritchie at Bell Labs between 1969 and 1973. C was designed for systems programming, particularly for writing the Unix operating system. C combines the efficiency of assembly language with the expressiveness of a high-level language. It provides low-level memory access through pointers while still offering structured control flow and a rich set of operators. The success of Unix made C incredibly influential. C++ was developed by Bjarne Stroustrup at Bell Labs in the 1980s as an extension of C that added classes, inheritance, polymorphism, and other object-oriented features. C++ became widely used for large-scale systems, games, and performance-critical applications. Java was developed by James Gosling at Sun Microsystems and released in 1995. Java was designed for cross-platform development through the Java Virtual Machine, or JVM, which executes bytecode that is independent of the underlying hardware. Java's slogan, write once run anywhere, appealed to developers building web applets and enterprise applications. Java also introduced automatic memory management through garbage collection, which reduced programmer errors related to memory allocation and deallocation. Python was created by Guido van Rossum and first released in 1991. Python emphasized code readability through significant whitespace, dynamic typing, and a comprehensive standard library. Python's simple syntax and interpreted nature made it popular for scripting, automation, and rapid prototyping. In the 2010s, Python became the dominant language for data science, machine learning, and scientific computing due to libraries such as NumPy, SciPy, pandas, scikit learn, and TensorFlow. JavaScript was created by Brendan Eich at Netscape in 1995 in just ten days. JavaScript was designed to add interactivity to web pages, and it became the standard scripting language for web browsers. With the development of Node.js in 2009, JavaScript expanded to server-side programming, and it is now one of the most widely used languages in the world. Ruby, designed by Yukihiro Matsumoto in the mid 1990s, emphasized developer happiness and elegant syntax. The Ruby on Rails web framework popularized convention over configuration and made web development more productive. Swift, developed by Apple and released in 2014, is designed for iOS and macOS development, offering safety features and modern syntax. Go, developed by Google and released in 2009, is designed for concurrent systems programming with built-in goroutines and channels. Rust, developed by Mozilla and released in 2015, offers memory safety without garbage collection through a borrow checker. The field of artificial intelligence formally began at the Dartmouth Summer Research Project in 1956, organized by John McCarthy, Marvin Minsky, Nathaniel Rochester, and Claude Shannon. The Dartmouth workshop brought together researchers interested in machines that could think, learn, and reason. Early AI research focused on symbolic reasoning, logic, and problem solving. Allen Newell and Herbert Simon developed the Logic Theorist, considered the first AI program, which proved theorems in propositional logic. They also developed the General Problem Solver, which attempted to solve a wide range of puzzles and problems using heuristic search. The early period of AI was optimistic. Researchers believed that intelligent systems were only a decade or two away. There were rapid advances in search algorithms, theorem proving, and natural language processing. Joseph Weizenbaum at MIT developed ELIZA in the 1960s, a program that simulated a Rogerian psychotherapist using simple pattern matching and substitution. ELIZA demonstrated how easily people attributed intelligence to even very simple conversational programs. Marvin Minsky and Seymour Papert wrote Perceptrons, a book that analyzed the limitations of single-layer neural networks. The book showed that perceptrons could not learn the exclusive or function, which led to a sharp decline in interest in neural networks. This contributed to the first AI winter in the 1970s, a period of reduced funding and pessimism due to the failure of early AI systems to deliver on their ambitious promises. Expert systems emerged in the 1970s and 1980s as a more successful approach to AI. Expert systems encoded human knowledge in rules of the form if condition then conclusion. MYCIN, developed at Stanford, diagnosed bacterial infections and recommended antibiotics. XCON, developed for Digital Equipment Corporation, helped configure VAX computer systems. Expert systems were commercially successful, leading to a resurgence of AI funding and interest in the 1980s, known as the second AI boom. However, expert systems were brittle, difficult to maintain, and required extensive manual knowledge engineering. The collapse of the Lisp machine market and the failure of the Fifth Generation Computer Project in Japan contributed to the second AI winter in the late 1980s and early 1990s. Machine learning, which focuses on algorithms that learn from data rather than explicitly programmed rules, became the dominant paradigm in AI starting in the 1990s. Decision trees, such as ID3 and C4.5 developed by Ross Quinlan, provided interpretable classification models. Support vector machines, developed by Vladimir Vapnik and others, achieved state of the art results on many tasks by finding hyperplanes that maximally separate classes. Ensemble methods such as random forests and gradient boosting machines combine many weak models to produce a strong model. Neural networks returned to prominence in the 2000s and 2010s due to three factors: large datasets, powerful graphics processing units or GPUs, and better training algorithms. Geoffrey Hinton, Yann LeCun, and Yoshua Bengio are considered the godfathers of deep learning. Hinton's group at University of Toronto showed that deep belief networks could be trained effectively. LeCun developed convolutional neural networks, or CNNs, for handwritten digit recognition and later for image classification. Bengio worked on recurrent neural networks and natural language processing. The ImageNet Large Scale Visual Recognition Challenge, launched in 2010, provided a benchmark for image classification. In 2012, the AlexNet architecture, designed by Alex Krizhevsky, Ilya Sutskever, and Geoffrey Hinton, achieved a dramatic reduction in error rate using a deep convolutional neural network trained on two GPUs. This breakthrough sparked the deep learning revolution. Convolutional neural networks became the standard for image recognition, object detection, and segmentation. Recurrent neural networks, especially long short-term memory or LSTM networks developed by Sepp Hochreiter and Jürgen Schmidhuber, excelled at sequence tasks such as machine translation, speech recognition, and handwriting generation. The attention mechanism, introduced for machine translation, allowed neural networks to focus on relevant parts of the input sequence. The Transformer architecture, introduced in the 2017 paper Attention Is All You Need by Vaswani and colleagues, replaced recurrence entirely with self-attention and position encodings. Transformers achieved state of the art results on many natural language processing tasks and became the foundation for large language models. Generative adversarial networks, or GANs, introduced by Ian Goodfellow in 2014, pit a generator against a discriminator in a game, producing realistic synthetic images, audio, and video. Variational autoencoders provide another approach to generative modeling. Reinforcement learning, where an agent learns to maximize reward through interaction with an environment, achieved superhuman performance in games such as Go, chess, and Atari. Deep Q-Networks, or DQN, developed by DeepMind, combined deep neural networks with Q-learning to learn directly from raw pixels. AlphaGo, also from DeepMind, defeated world champion Lee Sedol in Go, a game long considered beyond the reach of AI due to its enormous branching factor. AlphaZero generalized this approach to master multiple games, including chess, shogi, and Go, starting from only the rules and no human data. Large language models, or LLMs, are the most visible AI technology of the 2020s. These models are Transformers trained on massive text corpora containing billions or trillions of words. GPT, or Generative Pre trained Transformer, developed by OpenAI, showed that language models could generate coherent text, answer questions, summarize documents, and write code. GPT-3, released in 2020 with 175 billion parameters, demonstrated few-shot and zero-shot learning, performing tasks it was never explicitly trained on given only a few examples in the prompt. GPT-4 and subsequent models improved reasoning, factual accuracy, and safety. LLMs can exhibit emergent abilities such as in-context learning, chain of thought reasoning, and tool use. However, they also suffer from hallucinations, generating plausible but incorrect statements, and they can amplify biases present in their training data. Ethical concerns about AI include bias, fairness, transparency, accountability, privacy, job displacement, and the potential for misuse in surveillance, disinformation, and autonomous weapons. The alignment problem asks how to ensure that advanced AI systems pursue goals that are beneficial to humans. Interpretability research aims to understand how models arrive at their decisions, making them less of a black box. AI safety research includes robustness, avoiding reward hacking, specification gaming, and preventing unintended emergent behaviors. The future of AI may include multimodal models that process text, images, audio, and video together; agentic systems that take actions over extended time horizons; and continued scaling of models and data. Whether artificial general intelligence, defined as AI that matches or exceeds human performance across all economically valuable tasks, will be achieved and when remains an open debate. Some researchers predict AGI within this decade, while others argue that fundamental breakthroughs are still required. Regardless, artificial intelligence has already transformed fields from healthcare to transportation to entertainment, and its influence continues to grow.
