Organic chemistry is the branch of chemistry that studies carbon-containing compounds. Carbon is unique among the elements because it can form stable covalent bonds with itself and with many other elements, leading to an enormous diversity of molecules. Carbon has four valence electrons and typically forms four bonds, which can be single, double, or triple bonds. The simplest organic molecules are hydrocarbons, which contain only carbon and hydrogen. Methane is CH four, the simplest alkane, with one carbon bonded to four hydrogens in a tetrahedral geometry. Ethane is C two H six, with a carbon carbon single bond. Ethene, also called ethylene, is C two H four with a carbon carbon double bond, which is planar and rigid. Ethyne, also called acetylene, is C two H two with a carbon carbon triple bond, which is linear. Alkanes are saturated hydrocarbons, meaning all carbon carbon bonds are single bonds, and each carbon is bonded to the maximum number of hydrogens. Alkanes have the general formula C n H two n plus two. They are relatively unreactive except for combustion and halogenation reactions. Alkenes contain at least one carbon carbon double bond and have the general formula C n H two n. The double bond consists of one sigma bond and one pi bond. The pi bond restricts rotation, leading to geometric isomerism. In cis isomers, substituents are on the same side of the double bond, while in trans isomers, substituents are on opposite sides. Alkynes contain at least one carbon carbon triple bond, consisting of one sigma bond and two pi bonds, and have the general formula C n H two n minus two. Functional groups are specific arrangements of atoms within organic molecules that determine their chemical reactivity. The hydroxyl group, which is O H, characterizes alcohols. Methanol, ethanol, and isopropanol are common alcohols. The carbonyl group, which is carbon double bonded to oxygen, appears in several functional families. Aldehydes have the carbonyl group at the end of a carbon chain with at least one hydrogen attached to the carbonyl carbon. Ketones have the carbonyl group in the middle of a carbon chain with two carbon groups attached to the carbonyl carbon. Carboxylic acids contain the carboxyl group, which is a carbonyl group bonded to a hydroxyl group, written as C O O H. Acetic acid, found in vinegar, is a simple carboxylic acid. Esters are formed by the reaction of a carboxylic acid with an alcohol, releasing water, and they often have pleasant fruity smells. Amines contain nitrogen atoms bonded to carbon and hydrogen, and they behave as weak bases due to the lone pair of electrons on nitrogen. Amides are formed from carboxylic acids and amines, and they are important in biochemistry as the bonds linking amino acids in proteins. Ethers have an oxygen atom bonded to two carbon groups. Thiols are sulfur analogs of alcohols, with an S H group instead of O H, and they are responsible for the strong odors of skunks and garlic. Aromatic compounds contain a benzene ring, a six carbon ring with alternating single and double bonds. Benzene is unusually stable due to delocalization of its six pi electrons over the entire ring, a property called aromaticity. The electrons in the pi system are shared equally among all six carbons, making benzene less reactive than typical alkenes. Substituted benzenes have other functional groups attached to the ring. Phenol is benzene with a hydroxyl group. Aniline is benzene with an amino group. Benzoic acid is benzene with a carboxyl group. Electrophilic aromatic substitution is the characteristic reaction of benzene and its derivatives, where an electrophile replaces a hydrogen atom on the ring. Isomerism is a fundamental concept in organic chemistry. Structural isomers have the same molecular formula but different connectivity of atoms. For example, butane and isobutane both have the formula C four H ten, but butane has a straight chain of four carbons while isobutane has a branched chain with three carbons in a row and one carbon attached to the middle carbon. Stereoisomers have the same connectivity but different three dimensional arrangements. Enantiomers are non superimposable mirror images, like left and right hands. Enantiomers have identical physical properties such as melting point and boiling point, but they rotate plane polarized light in opposite directions and interact differently with other chiral molecules. Diastereomers are stereoisomers that are not mirror images. Chirality arises when a carbon atom has four different substituents. Such a carbon is called a stereocenter or chiral center. Many biological molecules are chiral, and often only one enantiomer is biologically active. For example, the drug thalidomide had one enantiomer that reduced morning sickness and another that caused severe birth defects. Organic reactions can be classified into several major types. Substitution reactions replace one atom or group with another. In an SN two reaction, a nucleophile attacks the carbon from the opposite side of the leaving group, causing inversion of configuration. In an SN one reaction, the leaving group departs first to form a carbocation intermediate, which is then attacked by the nucleophile, leading to racemization. Elimination reactions remove atoms or groups from adjacent carbons to form a double bond. In an E two elimination, a base removes a hydrogen from one carbon while a leaving group departs from the adjacent carbon in a single concerted step. In an E one elimination, the leaving group departs first to form a carbocation, followed by removal of a hydrogen by a base. Addition reactions add atoms or groups to a double or triple bond. In the addition of hydrogen halides to alkenes, Markovnikov's rule states that the hydrogen adds to the carbon with more hydrogens already attached, and the halide adds to the carbon with fewer hydrogens. In hydroboration oxidation, the anti Markovnikov addition of water occurs, placing the hydroxyl group on the less substituted carbon. Oxidation reduction reactions in organic chemistry involve changes in the number of carbon hydrogen or carbon oxygen bonds. Oxidation increases the number of bonds from carbon to oxygen or decreases bonds from carbon to hydrogen. Reduction does the opposite. Primary alcohols can be oxidized to aldehydes and then to carboxylic acids. Secondary alcohols can be oxidized to ketones. Tertiary alcohols are resistant to oxidation. Biochemistry applies organic chemistry principles to the molecules and reactions of living organisms. The four major classes of biomolecules are carbohydrates, lipids, proteins, and nucleic acids. Carbohydrates are polyhydroxy aldehydes or ketones. The simplest carbohydrates are monosaccharides, such as glucose, fructose, and galactose. Glucose is an aldohexose, containing six carbons and an aldehyde group. Fructose is a ketohexose, containing six carbons and a ketone group. Monosaccharides can exist as linear chains or as five membered or six membered rings. The cyclic forms involve a bond between the carbonyl carbon and a hydroxyl oxygen, creating a new stereocenter called the anomeric carbon. The alpha anomer has the hydroxyl group on the anomeric carbon pointing down, while the beta anomer has it pointing up. Disaccharides are formed from two monosaccharides linked by a glycosidic bond. Sucrose, table sugar, is glucose linked to fructose. Lactose, milk sugar, is glucose linked to galactose. Maltose, malt sugar, is two glucose units linked. Polysaccharides are long polymers of monosaccharide units. Starch is the storage polysaccharide in plants, consisting of amylose and amylopectin. Amylose is a linear chain of glucose with alpha one four linkages. Amylopectin has alpha one four linkages and alpha one six branches. Glycogen is the storage polysaccharide in animals and is highly branched with alpha one four and alpha one six linkages. Cellulose is a structural polysaccharide in plant cell walls, consisting of glucose with beta one four linkages, which humans cannot digest because we lack the enzyme cellulase. Lipids are hydrophobic or amphipathic molecules that are insoluble in water. Triglycerides, also called triacylglycerols, are esters of glycerol with three fatty acids. Fatty acids are long chain carboxylic acids, typically with twelve to twenty four carbons. Saturated fatty acids have no double bonds, are straight chains, and pack tightly together, so they are solid at room temperature. Unsaturated fatty acids have one or more cis double bonds, which create kinks in the chain, preventing tight packing, so they are liquid at room temperature. Trans fats, which have trans double bonds, are produced by partial hydrogenation of vegetable oils and are associated with cardiovascular disease. Phospholipids are similar to triglycerides but have one fatty acid replaced by a phosphate group and often a polar head group such as choline or serine. Phospholipids spontaneously form bilayers in water, with the hydrophobic tails facing inward and the hydrophilic heads facing outward. This bilayer structure is the foundation of all cell membranes. Steroids are lipids with a characteristic four ring fused structure. Cholesterol is a steroid that modulates membrane fluidity and serves as a precursor for steroid hormones such as testosterone, estrogen, and cortisol. Proteins are polymers of amino acids. There are twenty standard amino acids, each with a central alpha carbon bonded to an amino group, a carboxyl group, a hydrogen atom, and a unique side chain called the R group. Amino acids are linked by peptide bonds, which are amide bonds formed between the carboxyl group of one amino acid and the amino group of the next. A dipeptide has two amino acids, a tripeptide has three, and a polypeptide has many. Polypeptides with a specific function are called proteins. The primary structure of a protein is the linear sequence of amino acids. The secondary structure refers to local folded structures stabilized by hydrogen bonds between backbone amide and carbonyl groups. The alpha helix is a right handed coil where each turn contains approximately three point six amino acids, and hydrogen bonds form between the carbonyl of residue n and the amide of residue n plus four. The beta sheet consists of extended strands connected by hydrogen bonds. Beta sheets can be parallel, with strands running in the same direction, or antiparallel, with strands running in opposite directions. The tertiary structure is the overall three dimensional folding of a single polypeptide chain, stabilized by interactions between side chains, including hydrophobic interactions, hydrogen bonds, ionic bonds, and disulfide bridges between cysteine residues. The quaternary structure involves the assembly of multiple polypeptide subunits into a functional protein. Hemoglobin, for example, has four subunits. Protein folding is a spontaneous process driven by the hydrophobic effect, which causes nonpolar side chains to cluster in the interior of the protein away from water. Chaperone proteins assist in proper folding and prevent aggregation. Misfolded proteins can cause diseases such as Alzheimer's, Parkinson's, and prion disorders. Enzymes are proteins that catalyze biochemical reactions. They lower the activation energy of a reaction without being consumed. The molecule that an enzyme acts upon is called the substrate. The active site is a specific pocket or cleft in the enzyme where the substrate binds. The lock and key model proposes that the active site is complementary in shape to the substrate. The induced fit model proposes that the active site molds itself around the substrate upon binding. Enzymes use several mechanisms to catalyze reactions: acid base catalysis, covalent catalysis, metal ion catalysis, and proximity and orientation effects. The rate of an enzyme catalyzed reaction follows the Michaelis Menten equation, which relates reaction velocity to substrate concentration. V max is the maximum reaction velocity when the enzyme is saturated with substrate. K m is the Michaelis constant, equal to the substrate concentration at half V max, and it reflects the affinity of the enzyme for its substrate. Enzyme inhibitors reduce enzyme activity. Competitive inhibitors bind to the active site and compete with substrate, increasing the apparent K m but not changing V max. Noncompetitive inhibitors bind to a different site, reducing V max without changing K m. Uncompetitive inhibitors bind only to the enzyme substrate complex, reducing both V max and K m. Allosteric regulation occurs when a molecule binds to a site other than the active site, causing a conformational change that affects activity. Allosteric enzymes often have multiple subunits and show sigmoidal kinetics rather than hyperbolic kinetics. Nucleic acids store and transmit genetic information. DNA, or deoxyribonucleic acid, is a polymer of nucleotides. Each nucleotide consists of a deoxyribose sugar, a phosphate group, and one of four nitrogenous bases: adenine, guanine, cytosine, or thymine. RNA, or ribonucleic acid, uses ribose sugar instead of deoxyribose and uracil instead of thymine. The structure of DNA was determined by James Watson and Francis Crick in 1953, using X ray diffraction data from Rosalind Franklin and Maurice Wilkins. DNA is a double helix with two antiparallel strands held together by hydrogen bonds between complementary base pairs. Adenine pairs with thymine via two hydrogen bonds, and guanine pairs with cytosine via three hydrogen bonds. The two strands are oriented in opposite directions, with one running five prime to three prime and the other running three prime to five prime. The double helix has a major groove and a minor groove, which are important for protein binding. DNA replication is semiconservative, meaning each new DNA molecule contains one old strand and one newly synthesized strand. Replication begins at origins of replication where the double helix unwinds. Helicase unwinds the DNA, and single strand binding proteins prevent reannealing. Primase synthesizes a short RNA primer, which provides a three prime hydroxyl group for DNA polymerase to add nucleotides. DNA polymerase synthesizes the new strand in the five prime to three prime direction. On the leading strand, synthesis is continuous. On the lagging strand, synthesis is discontinuous, producing Okazaki fragments that are later joined by DNA ligase. Transcription is the process of synthesizing RNA from a DNA template. RNA polymerase binds to a promoter region near a gene and separates the DNA strands. It adds ribonucleotides complementary to the DNA template strand, synthesizing RNA in the five prime to three prime direction. In bacteria, transcription produces messenger RNA, or mRNA, which is directly translated. In eukaryotes, the initial transcript, called pre mRNA, undergoes processing including the addition of a five prime cap, addition of a poly A tail, and splicing to remove introns. Introns are noncoding sequences, while exons are coding sequences. Splicing is carried out by the spliceosome, a complex of small nuclear RNAs and proteins. Alternative splicing allows a single gene to produce multiple different protein isoforms by combining exons in different ways. Translation is the synthesis of a polypeptide chain using the information in mRNA. Translation occurs on ribosomes, which are complexes of ribosomal RNA and proteins. Transfer RNA, or tRNA, molecules carry specific amino acids and have anticodons that base pair with codons on the mRNA. The genetic code is a set of three nucleotide codons that specify each of the twenty amino acids. The code is degenerate, meaning most amino acids are specified by more than one codon. The code is nearly universal across all life forms, indicating a common evolutionary origin. Translation begins with initiation. The small ribosomal subunit binds to the mRNA near the start codon, which is AUG, coding for methionine. The initiator tRNA carrying methionine binds to the start codon. The large ribosomal subunit then joins, forming a complete ribosome with three sites: the A site for aminoacyl tRNA, the P site for peptidyl tRNA, and the E site for exit. During elongation, the ribosome reads each codon, and a matching tRNA brings its amino acid to the A site. A peptide bond forms between the new amino acid and the growing polypeptide chain, transferring the chain from the tRNA in the P site to the tRNA in the A site. The ribosome then translocates, moving the tRNA from the A site to the P site and the deacylated tRNA from the P site to the E site, where it exits. This process repeats until a stop codon, such as UAA, UAG, or UGA, is reached. Release factors bind to the stop codon, causing the release of the completed polypeptide and dissociation of the ribosome. The central dogma of molecular biology, formulated by Francis Crick, describes the flow of genetic information as DNA to RNA to protein. Exceptions include reverse transcription, where RNA is used as a template for DNA synthesis, as in retroviruses like HIV. Some RNA molecules can catalyze reactions, as in ribozymes. The regulation of gene expression controls when and how much of a protein is produced. In bacteria, the lac operon is a classic model. The lac operon contains genes for lactose metabolism, and it is regulated by a repressor protein that binds to the operator in the absence of lactose, preventing transcription. When lactose is present, it is converted to allolactose, which binds to the repressor and changes its shape, causing it to dissociate from the operator, allowing transcription. In eukaryotes, gene regulation is more complex and occurs at multiple levels. Chromatin structure affects gene accessibility. Acetylation of histones generally increases transcription by loosening DNA histone interactions. Methylation of DNA at CpG islands is associated with gene silencing. Transcription factors are proteins that bind to specific DNA sequences near genes to activate or repress transcription. Enhancers are distant regulatory sequences that can increase transcription even if located thousands of base pairs away from the promoter. MicroRNAs are small noncoding RNAs that bind to complementary sequences in target mRNAs, leading to mRNA degradation or translational repression. Molecular biology techniques have revolutionized biological research. Polymerase chain reaction, or PCR, amplifies specific DNA sequences exponentially. PCR uses a heat stable DNA polymerase, primers complementary to the target sequence, and repeated cycles of denaturation, annealing, and extension. Gel electrophoresis separates DNA, RNA, or proteins by size. DNA sequencing determines the exact order of nucleotides in a DNA molecule. Sanger sequencing uses chain terminating dideoxynucleotides. Next generation sequencing methods allow massively parallel sequencing of millions of DNA fragments. Recombinant DNA technology combines DNA from different sources. Restriction enzymes cut DNA at specific sequences, producing fragments that can be joined using DNA ligase. Plasmids are small circular DNA molecules that can serve as vectors to carry foreign DNA into bacteria. Transgenic organisms have been engineered to express foreign genes. CRISPR Cas9 is a powerful gene editing tool derived from a bacterial immune system. The Cas9 protein, guided by a short RNA molecule, cuts DNA at a specific sequence. This cut can be repaired by nonhomologous end joining, which often disrupts the gene, or by homology directed repair using a provided DNA template to introduce specific changes. CRISPR has applications in basic research, agriculture, and medicine, including potential gene therapies for genetic diseases. The study of metabolism encompasses all chemical reactions that occur in living organisms. Catabolic pathways break down complex molecules into simpler ones, releasing energy. Anabolic pathways build complex molecules from simpler ones, requiring energy. ATP, or adenosine triphosphate, is the universal energy currency of the cell. Hydrolysis of ATP to ADP and inorganic phosphate releases energy that drives many cellular processes. Glycolysis is a ten step pathway that breaks down glucose into two molecules of pyruvate, producing a net gain of two ATP and two NADH. Glycolysis occurs in the cytoplasm and does not require oxygen. The citric acid cycle, also called the Krebs cycle, oxidizes acetyl CoA to carbon dioxide, producing NADH, FADH two, and GTP. The cycle occurs in the mitochondrial matrix in eukaryotes. Oxidative phosphorylation uses the electron transport chain to transfer electrons from NADH and FADH two to oxygen, creating a proton gradient across the inner mitochondrial membrane. ATP synthase uses the energy of this proton gradient to synthesize ATP. The complete oxidation of one glucose molecule yields approximately thirty to thirty two ATP. Photosynthesis converts light energy into chemical energy. Light dependent reactions occur in the thylakoid membranes of chloroplasts. Chlorophyll and other pigments absorb light, exciting electrons that travel through two photosystems and an electron transport chain, producing ATP and NADPH. Water is split to replace the lost electrons, releasing oxygen. The Calvin cycle uses ATP and NADPH to fix carbon dioxide into organic molecules, specifically producing glyceraldehyde three phosphate, which can be converted to glucose and other carbohydrates. The enzyme Rubisco catalyzes the first step of carbon fixation, combining carbon dioxide with ribulose bisphosphate. Rubisco is often considered the most abundant protein on Earth.
