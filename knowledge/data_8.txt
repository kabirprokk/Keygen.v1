
CELL BIOLOGY

Cell theory is one of the fundamental principles of biology. It states three main points. First, all living organisms are composed of one or more cells. Second, the cell is the basic unit of structure and organization in organisms. Third, all cells arise from pre existing cells through cell division. There are two primary types of cells. Prokaryotic cells lack a nucleus and membrane bound organelles. Their DNA is located in a region called the nucleoid. Bacteria and archaea are prokaryotes. Eukaryotic cells have a nucleus that contains the cell's genetic material and membrane bound organelles. Plants, animals, fungi, and protists are eukaryotes. The cell membrane, also called the plasma membrane, surrounds all cells. It is composed of a phospholipid bilayer with embedded proteins. The phospholipids have hydrophilic heads that face the watery environments inside and outside the cell and hydrophobic tails that face each other in the interior of the membrane. This structure is described by the fluid mosaic model because the membrane is fluid and the components can move laterally. The cell membrane regulates what enters and exits the cell through selective permeability. Small nonpolar molecules like oxygen and carbon dioxide diffuse across the membrane easily. Water and small polar molecules cross more slowly. Ions and large polar molecules like glucose require transport proteins. The nucleus is the control center of the eukaryotic cell. It is surrounded by the nuclear envelope, a double membrane with nuclear pores that control traffic in and out. Inside the nucleus is chromatin, which is DNA wrapped around histone proteins. When a cell prepares to divide, chromatin condenses into chromosomes. The nucleolus is a region inside the nucleus where ribosomal RNA is synthesized and ribosomes begin to assemble. Ribosomes are the sites of protein synthesis. They are composed of ribosomal RNA and protein. Ribosomes can be free in the cytoplasm, where they make proteins that function in the cytosol, or bound to the endoplasmic reticulum, where they make proteins destined for secretion or for membranes. The endoplasmic reticulum or ER is a network of membranes. Rough ER has ribosomes attached and is involved in protein synthesis and processing. Smooth ER lacks ribosomes and is involved in lipid synthesis, detoxification, and calcium storage. The Golgi apparatus receives proteins and lipids from the ER, modifies them, sorts them, and packages them into vesicles for transport to other parts of the cell or for secretion. Lysosomes are membrane bound vesicles containing digestive enzymes. They break down worn out organelles, bacteria, and other materials. Peroxisomes contain enzymes that break down fatty acids and detoxify harmful substances like hydrogen peroxide. Mitochondria are the powerhouses of the cell. They generate adenosine triphosphate or ATP through cellular respiration. Mitochondria have a double membrane. The inner membrane is folded into cristae to increase surface area. The fluid inside the mitochondria is the matrix. Mitochondria contain their own DNA and ribosomes, supporting the endosymbiotic theory that they were once free living bacteria. Chloroplasts are found in plant cells and some protists. They perform photosynthesis, converting light energy into chemical energy stored in glucose. Chloroplasts also have a double membrane and their own DNA. Inside are stacks of thylakoids called grana, and the fluid surrounding them is the stroma. The cytoskeleton is a network of protein fibers that provides structural support, enables cell movement, and transports materials within the cell. Microtubules are hollow tubes made of tubulin. They form the spindle during cell division and serve as tracks for motor proteins. Microfilaments are thin fibers of actin. They are involved in cell contraction, crawling, and maintaining cell shape. Intermediate filaments provide mechanical strength. Plant cells have additional structures not found in animal cells. The cell wall, made of cellulose, surrounds the plasma membrane and provides rigid support. Central vacuoles store water, nutrients, and waste products and help maintain turgor pressure. Plasmodesmata are channels through the cell wall that allow communication and transport between adjacent plant cells. Cell division occurs through mitosis and cytokinesis. Mitosis produces two genetically identical daughter cells from one parent cell. It consists of prophase, metaphase, anaphase, and telophase. Prophase condenses chromosomes and the nuclear envelope breaks down. Metaphase aligns chromosomes at the metaphase plate. Anaphase separates sister chromatids to opposite poles. Telophase reforms the nuclear envelope around each set of chromosomes. Cytokinesis divides the cytoplasm. In animal cells, a cleavage furrow pinches the cell in two. In plant cells, a cell plate forms to become the new cell wall. Meiosis produces gametes with half the number of chromosomes. It involves two rounds of division and results in four genetically unique haploid cells. Crossing over during prophase I exchanges genetic material between homologous chromosomes, increasing genetic diversity. Cellular respiration is the process by which cells break down glucose to produce ATP. It occurs in three main stages. Glycolysis occurs in the cytoplasm and breaks glucose into two pyruvate molecules, producing a net gain of two ATP and two NADH. The Krebs cycle, also called the citric acid cycle, occurs in the mitochondrial matrix. It completes the breakdown of glucose, producing carbon dioxide, ATP, NADH, and FADH2. The electron transport chain is located on the inner mitochondrial membrane. It uses the high energy electrons from NADH and FADH2 to pump protons across the membrane, creating a gradient. ATP synthase uses the flow of protons back across the membrane to generate ATP. Oxygen is the final electron acceptor, combining with electrons and hydrogen ions to form water. Fermentation occurs when oxygen is not available. It regenerates NAD plus so glycolysis can continue. Lactic acid fermentation produces lactate, as in muscle cells during intense exercise. Alcoholic fermentation produces ethanol and carbon dioxide, as in yeast. Photosynthesis occurs in chloroplasts and converts light energy into chemical energy. The overall equation is carbon dioxide plus water plus light energy produces glucose and oxygen. The light dependent reactions occur in the thylakoid membranes. Chlorophyll absorbs light, exciting electrons that travel through an electron transport chain, producing ATP and NADPH. Water is split, releasing oxygen. The Calvin cycle occurs in the stroma. It uses ATP and NADPH to fix carbon dioxide into organic molecules, specifically producing glucose. The enzyme Rubisco catalyzes the first step of carbon fixation.

INTRODUCTORY CHEMISTRY

Chemistry is the study of matter, its properties, how substances combine or separate, and how they interact with energy. Matter is anything that has mass and takes up space. All matter is composed of atoms. An atom is the smallest unit of an element that retains the chemical properties of that element. Atoms consist of a nucleus containing protons and neutrons, with electrons orbiting the nucleus in electron clouds. Protons have a positive electric charge. Neutrons have no charge, they are neutral. Electrons have a negative electric charge. The number of protons in an atom is the atomic number, which determines the element. For example, all hydrogen atoms have one proton, all carbon atoms have six protons, and all oxygen atoms have eight protons. The mass number is the total number of protons plus neutrons. Isotopes are atoms of the same element with different numbers of neutrons. For example, carbon twelve has six protons and six neutrons, while carbon fourteen has six protons and eight neutrons. Some isotopes are radioactive, meaning their nuclei are unstable and decay over time, emitting radiation. Electrons occupy energy levels or shells around the nucleus. The first shell can hold up to two electrons. The second shell can hold up to eight electrons. The third shell can hold up to eighteen electrons, but for the first eighteen elements, it holds eight. The chemical behavior of an atom is determined by the number of electrons in its outermost shell, called valence electrons. Atoms tend to gain, lose, or share electrons to achieve a full outer shell of eight electrons, which is the octet rule. When atoms gain or lose electrons, they become ions. An atom that loses an electron becomes a positively charged cation. An atom that gains an electron becomes a negatively charged anion. Ionic bonds form when electrons are transferred from one atom to another, creating oppositely charged ions that attract each other. For example, sodium loses one electron to become Na plus, and chlorine gains that electron to become Cl minus, forming sodium chloride or table salt. Covalent bonds form when atoms share electrons. A single covalent bond shares one pair of electrons. A double bond shares two pairs. A triple bond shares three pairs. Water, or H two O, has two single covalent bonds between oxygen and hydrogen. Methane, or C H four, has four single covalent bonds. Oxygen gas, or O two, has a double bond between the two oxygen atoms. Nitrogen gas, or N two, has a triple bond. Molecules are groups of atoms held together by covalent bonds. Compounds are substances made of two or more different elements chemically bonded. Water is a compound. Oxygen gas is a molecule but not a compound because it contains only one element. The periodic table arranges all known elements by atomic number. Rows are called periods. Columns are called groups. Elements in the same group have similar chemical properties because they have the same number of valence electrons. Group one elements, the alkali metals, are highly reactive metals that lose one electron. Group two elements, the alkaline earth metals, lose two electrons. Group seventeen elements, the halogens, are highly reactive nonmetals that gain one electron. Group eighteen elements, the noble gases, have full outer shells and are chemically inert. Chemical reactions involve the rearrangement of atoms to form new substances. Reactants are the starting substances, and products are the substances formed. The law of conservation of mass states that matter is neither created nor destroyed in a chemical reaction, so the number of atoms of each element must be the same on both sides of a balanced chemical equation. For example, the reaction of hydrogen and oxygen to form water is written as two H two plus O two produces two H two O. There are four hydrogen atoms and two oxygen atoms on each side. Chemical reactions can be classified into several types. Synthesis reactions combine two or more substances to form a more complex product. Decomposition reactions break a compound into simpler substances. Single replacement reactions swap one element for another in a compound. Double replacement reactions exchange ions between two compounds. Combustion reactions rapidly combine a fuel with oxygen, releasing heat and light, and typically produce carbon dioxide and water. Acids and bases are important classes of chemicals. According to the Arrhenius definition, an acid increases the concentration of hydrogen ions, H plus, in water. A base increases the concentration of hydroxide ions, O H minus. According to the Brønsted Lowry definition, an acid donates a proton, and a base accepts a proton. The pH scale measures how acidic or basic a solution is. It ranges from zero to fourteen. A pH of seven is neutral, pure water. A pH less than seven is acidic, with lower numbers being more acidic. A pH greater than seven is basic or alkaline, with higher numbers being more basic. The pH is the negative logarithm of the hydrogen ion concentration. A change of one pH unit represents a tenfold change in hydrogen ion concentration. Buffers are solutions that resist changes in pH when small amounts of acid or base are added. They are important in biological systems, such as blood, which must maintain a pH near seven point four. Chemical bonds store energy. Energy is released when bonds form, and energy is required to break bonds. In exothermic reactions, more energy is released when new bonds form than is required to break the original bonds, so the reaction releases heat. In endothermic reactions, more energy is required to break bonds than is released when new bonds form, so the reaction absorbs heat. Catalysts are substances that speed up chemical reactions without being consumed. Enzymes are biological catalysts, usually proteins, that are essential for life. They lower the activation energy required for a reaction to proceed. The state of matter depends on temperature and pressure. Solids have a fixed shape and volume because particles are tightly packed and only vibrate in place. Liquids have a fixed volume but take the shape of their container because particles can slide past each other. Gases have no fixed shape or volume and expand to fill their container because particles move rapidly and are far apart. Plasma is the fourth state of matter, consisting of ionized gas with free electrons and ions. It is found in stars, lightning, and fluorescent lights. Phase changes occur when matter changes state. Melting is solid to liquid. Freezing is liquid to solid. Vaporization is liquid to gas. Condensation is gas to liquid. Sublimation is solid to gas directly, like dry ice. Deposition is gas to solid directly, like frost. These phase changes occur at specific temperatures for each substance under standard pressure, such as zero degrees Celsius for the melting and freezing point of water and one hundred degrees Celsius for the boiling point of water.

WORLD GEOGRAPHY

World geography is the study of the Earth's physical features, climates, populations, cultures, and the relationships between people and their environments. Physical geography focuses on natural features. Human geography focuses on human activity and its relationship to the Earth. The Earth is approximately spherical, slightly flattened at the poles, with a circumference of about forty thousand kilometers. The Earth rotates on its axis once every twenty four hours, causing day and night, and revolves around the sun once every three hundred sixty five point two five days, causing the seasons. The equator is an imaginary line that circles the Earth at zero degrees latitude, equidistant from the North and South Poles. It divides the Earth into the Northern Hemisphere and the Southern Hemisphere. Latitude lines, also called parallels, run east west and measure distance north or south of the equator. The equator is zero degrees latitude. The Tropic of Cancer is at approximately twenty three point five degrees north latitude. The Tropic of Capricorn is at approximately twenty three point five degrees south latitude. The Arctic Circle is at approximately sixty six point five degrees north latitude. The Antarctic Circle is at approximately sixty six point five degrees south latitude. The North Pole is at ninety degrees north latitude. The South Pole is at ninety degrees south latitude. Longitude lines, also called meridians, run north south and measure distance east or west of the Prime Meridian. The Prime Meridian is at zero degrees longitude and passes through Greenwich, England. The International Date Line is approximately at one hundred eighty degrees longitude. When crossing the International Date Line going west, you advance one day. Going east, you go back one day. The Earth's surface is about seventy one percent water and twenty nine percent land. The five major oceans, from largest to smallest, are the Pacific Ocean, Atlantic Ocean, Indian Ocean, Southern Ocean, and Arctic Ocean. The Pacific Ocean is the deepest and covers about thirty two percent of the Earth's surface. The seven continents, from largest to smallest by land area, are Asia, Africa, North America, South America, Antarctica, Europe, and Australia. Asia is the largest and most populous continent, containing China, India, Russia, and the Middle East. Africa is the second largest, containing the Sahara Desert, the world's largest hot desert, and the Nile River, the world's longest river. North America includes Canada, the United States, Mexico, and Central America. South America includes the Amazon Rainforest, the world's largest rainforest, and the Andes Mountains, the longest mountain range in the world. Antarctica is the coldest, driest, and windiest continent, covered by ice sheets. Europe is a continent of peninsulas, extending from Asia. Australia is the smallest continent, a country and a continent. Major mountain ranges include the Himalayas in Asia, containing Mount Everest, the highest peak on Earth at eight thousand eight hundred forty eight meters. The Andes in South America are the longest mountain range. The Rocky Mountains in North America. The Alps in Europe. The Urals in Europe and Asia form part of the boundary between the two continents. Major rivers include the Nile in Africa, the longest river. The Amazon in South America, the largest by volume. The Yangtze in China. The Mississippi Missouri in North America. The Yenisei in Siberia. The Ganges in India. The Danube in Europe. Major deserts include the Sahara in North Africa, the Arabian Desert in the Middle East, the Gobi Desert in Asia, the Kalahari Desert in southern Africa, the Great Victoria Desert in Australia, and the Atacama Desert in South America, which is the driest nonpolar desert. Major lakes include the Caspian Sea, which is actually a salt lake and the largest inland body of water on Earth. Lake Superior in North America is the largest freshwater lake by surface area. Lake Baikal in Siberia is the deepest and oldest freshwater lake. The Great Lakes of North America are Superior, Michigan, Huron, Erie, and Ontario. Climate zones are determined by temperature and precipitation. Tropical climates are near the equator, hot and humid year round, with rainforests. Dry climates include deserts and steppes with little rainfall. Temperate climates have four distinct seasons, moderate temperatures, and occur in the mid latitudes. Continental climates have cold winters and warm summers, found in the interiors of large landmasses. Polar climates are extremely cold year round, found near the poles. Mediterranean climates have dry summers and mild wet winters. The Köppen climate classification system divides climates into these categories. Biomes are large geographical areas defined by their climate and the plants and animals that live there. Tropical rainforests are near the equator with high rainfall and biodiversity. Temperate forests have deciduous trees that lose their leaves in winter. Taiga, or boreal forest, is a coniferous forest in cold northern regions. Grasslands include prairies in North America, steppes in Eurasia, and savannas in Africa with scattered trees. Deserts have very little rainfall, extreme temperature variations. Tundra is cold, treeless, with permafrost, frozen ground. Chaparral has shrubs and dry summers. Freshwater biomes include lakes, rivers, and wetlands. Marine biomes include oceans, coral reefs, and estuaries. Population geography studies human populations. The world population exceeded eight billion people in 2022. The most populous countries are China, India, the United States, Indonesia, Pakistan, Nigeria, Brazil, Bangladesh, Russia, and Mexico. Population density is the number of people per unit area. The most densely populated regions include South Asia, East Asia, and Western Europe. Sparsely populated regions include deserts, tundras, and rainforests. Migration is the movement of people from one place to another. Push factors drive people to leave, such as war, famine, poverty, or natural disasters. Pull factors attract people to a new place, such as jobs, safety, freedom, or family. Urbanization is the movement of people from rural areas to cities. Megacities are cities with over ten million people. The largest cities include Tokyo, Delhi, Shanghai, Sao Paulo, Mexico City, Cairo, Dhaka, Mumbai, Beijing, and Osaka. Cultural geography studies the distribution of languages, religions, and customs. Major language families include Indo European, which includes English, Spanish, Hindi, and Russian. Sino Tibetan includes Mandarin Chinese. Afroasiatic includes Arabic. Major religions include Christianity, largest, with branches of Catholicism, Protestantism, and Orthodoxy. Islam, second largest, with branches of Sunni and Shia. Hinduism, concentrated in India and Nepal. Buddhism, with branches of Theravada and Mahayana. Judaism. And non religious or atheist populations. Economic geography studies the distribution of resources and economic activities. Primary economic activities include agriculture, mining, fishing, and forestry. Secondary activities include manufacturing and construction. Tertiary activities include services like retail, banking, and education. Quaternary activities include information technology, research, and consulting. Developed countries, also called high income countries, include the United States, Canada, Western Europe, Japan, Australia, and South Korea. Developing countries, also called low and middle income countries, include much of Africa, Asia, and Latin America. Least developed countries face the greatest economic challenges. Globalization is the increasing interconnectedness of economies, cultures, and populations. It is driven by trade, travel, technology, and communication. Geographic information systems, or GIS, are computer systems that capture, store, analyze, and display geographic data. Remote sensing uses satellites and aircraft to collect data about the Earth's surface. Global Positioning System, or GPS, uses satellites to determine precise locations on Earth.

FUNDAMENTAL PHYSICS

Physics is the natural science that studies matter, its motion and behavior through space and time, and the related entities of energy and force. Classical mechanics describes the motion of macroscopic objects. Kinematics is the description of motion without considering its causes. Displacement is the change in position of an object. Velocity is the rate of change of displacement. Speed is the magnitude of velocity, without direction. Acceleration is the rate of change of velocity. The equations of motion for constant acceleration relate initial velocity, final velocity, acceleration, time, and displacement. For an object in free fall near Earth's surface, acceleration due to gravity is approximately nine point eight meters per second squared downward. Dynamics considers the forces that cause motion. Newton's first law states that an object at rest stays at rest and an object in motion stays in motion with constant velocity unless acted upon by a net external force. This is also called the law of inertia. Newton's second law states that the net force on an object equals its mass times its acceleration. Force is measured in newtons. One newton is the force needed to accelerate a one kilogram mass at one meter per second squared. Newton's third law states that for every action force, there is an equal and opposite reaction force. If object A exerts a force on object B, then object B exerts a force of equal magnitude but opposite direction on object A. Gravity is a fundamental force. On Earth, weight is the gravitational force on an object, equal to mass times the acceleration due to gravity. Weight is measured in newtons, while mass is measured in kilograms. Mass is the amount of matter in an object and is constant. Weight changes with gravity. Work is done when a force causes a displacement in the direction of the force. Work equals force times displacement. The unit of work is the joule. Energy is the capacity to do work. Kinetic energy is the energy of motion, equal to one half times mass times velocity squared. Potential energy is stored energy. Gravitational potential energy near Earth's surface equals mass times gravity times height. Elastic potential energy is stored in a stretched spring, equal to one half times the spring constant times the displacement squared. The law of conservation of energy states that the total energy in an isolated system remains constant. Energy can transform from one form to another but cannot be created or destroyed. Power is the rate at which work is done or energy is transferred. Power equals work divided by time and is measured in watts. One watt is one joule per second. Momentum is the product of mass and velocity. The law of conservation of momentum states that the total momentum of a closed system remains constant if no external forces act. In collisions, momentum is always conserved. In elastic collisions, kinetic energy is also conserved. In inelastic collisions, kinetic energy is not conserved, and some is converted to heat or sound. Rotational motion analogs exist for linear quantities. Angular displacement replaces linear displacement. Angular velocity replaces linear velocity. Angular acceleration replaces linear acceleration. Torque replaces force. Torque is the rotational effect of a force and equals force times lever arm distance. Moment of inertia replaces mass. Angular momentum is the product of moment of inertia and angular velocity, and it is conserved if no external torque acts. Thermodynamics deals with heat and temperature. Temperature is a measure of the average kinetic energy of the particles in a substance. It is measured in degrees Celsius, degrees Fahrenheit, or kelvins. The Kelvin scale is absolute, with zero kelvins being absolute zero, the coldest possible temperature where particles have minimum kinetic energy. Heat is the transfer of thermal energy between substances due to a temperature difference. Conduction is heat transfer through direct contact. Convection is heat transfer by the movement of fluids. Radiation is heat transfer by electromagnetic waves. Specific heat capacity is the amount of heat required to raise the temperature of one kilogram of a substance by one degree Celsius. The heat equation is heat equals mass times specific heat capacity times change in temperature. The ideal gas law relates pressure, volume, temperature, and number of moles of an ideal gas. It is written as pressure times volume equals number of moles times the universal gas constant times temperature. Waves are disturbances that transfer energy without transferring matter. Mechanical waves require a medium, like sound waves in air or water waves in water. Electromagnetic waves do not require a medium and can travel through a vacuum. Transverse waves have oscillations perpendicular to the direction of wave travel. Light waves are transverse. Longitudinal waves have oscillations parallel to the direction of wave travel. Sound waves are longitudinal. Wavelength is the distance between successive crests or troughs. Frequency is the number of cycles per second, measured in hertz. Period is the time for one complete cycle. The wave speed equation is speed equals frequency times wavelength. The speed of sound in air at room temperature is approximately three hundred forty three meters per second. The speed of light in a vacuum is approximately three hundred million meters per second, exactly two hundred ninety nine million seven hundred ninety two thousand four hundred fifty eight meters per second. Reflection occurs when a wave bounces off a surface. Refraction is the bending of a wave as it passes from one medium to another due to a change in speed. Diffraction is the spreading of waves around obstacles or through openings. Interference is the superposition of two or more waves. Constructive interference occurs when waves are in phase, amplitudes add, producing a larger wave. Destructive interference occurs when waves are out of phase, amplitudes cancel, producing a smaller wave or silence. The Doppler effect is the change in frequency of a wave for an observer moving relative to its source. A sound source moving toward an observer produces a higher frequency, higher pitch. Moving away produces a lower frequency, lower pitch. Electricity is the flow of electric charge. Electric current is the rate of flow of charge, measured in amperes. Voltage is the electric potential difference that drives current, measured in volts. Resistance opposes the flow of current, measured in ohms. Ohm's law states that current equals voltage divided by resistance. In a series circuit, components are connected end to end. The current is the same through all components. The total resistance is the sum of individual resistances. The voltage divides across components. In a parallel circuit, components are connected across the same voltage source. The voltage is the same across all components. The total current is the sum of the currents through each branch. The total resistance is less than the smallest individual resistance. Electric power is the rate at which electrical energy is transferred, equal to voltage times current, measured in watts. Magnetism is the force exerted by magnets. A magnetic field is the region around a magnet where magnetic forces act. Magnetic field lines point from north pole to south pole outside the magnet. An electric current produces a magnetic field. A moving charge in a magnetic field experiences a force perpendicular to both its velocity and the magnetic field. Electromagnetic induction is the production of an electric field by a changing magnetic field, which is the principle behind generators and transformers.
