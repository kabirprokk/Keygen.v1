Geology is the scientific study of the solid Earth, its composition, structure, physical properties, history, and the processes that shape it. The Earth is approximately four point five four billion years old, formed through the accretion of dust and gas in the early solar system. The interior of the Earth is layered, consisting of the crust, mantle, outer core, and inner core. The crust is the outermost solid shell, ranging from five to seventy kilometers in thickness. Oceanic crust is thinner and denser, composed primarily of basalt. Continental crust is thicker and less dense, composed primarily of granite. The mantle lies beneath the crust and extends to a depth of about two thousand nine hundred kilometers. The mantle is composed mostly of silicate rocks rich in iron and magnesium. Although solid, the mantle behaves as a very viscous fluid over geological timescales, undergoing slow convection currents that drive plate tectonics. The outer core extends from about two thousand nine hundred to five thousand one hundred fifty kilometers depth. It is composed of liquid iron and nickel, and the motion of this electrically conductive fluid generates Earth's magnetic field through the dynamo effect. The inner core is a solid sphere of iron and nickel with a radius of about one thousand two hundred twenty kilometers. Despite temperatures exceeding five thousand degrees Celsius, the inner core remains solid due to the immense pressure from the overlying layers. Plate tectonics is the unifying theory of geology. It describes how the Earth's lithosphere, which includes the crust and the uppermost rigid part of the mantle, is broken into several large and many small tectonic plates. These plates move relative to one another at rates of a few centimeters per year, about the speed at which fingernails grow. Plate boundaries are classified into three types. Divergent boundaries occur where plates move apart. At mid ocean ridges, upwelling mantle material partially melts to form new oceanic crust as the plates separate. The Mid Atlantic Ridge is an example of a divergent boundary. Convergent boundaries occur where plates move toward each other. When an oceanic plate collides with a continental plate, the denser oceanic plate subducts beneath the continental plate, descending into the mantle. Subduction zones are associated with deep ocean trenches, volcanic arcs, and powerful earthquakes. The Pacific Ring of Fire, which encircles the Pacific Ocean, is the most seismically and volcanically active region on Earth, with numerous subduction zones. When two continental plates converge, neither subducts easily due to their low density, resulting in mountain building, or orogeny. The collision of the Indian Plate with the Eurasian Plate created the Himalayan mountain range, including Mount Everest, the highest peak on Earth. Transform boundaries occur where plates slide past each other horizontally. The San Andreas Fault in California is a classic example of a transform boundary, where the Pacific Plate moves northwest relative to the North American Plate. Earthquakes are the shaking of the Earth's surface caused by the sudden release of energy in the lithosphere. Most earthquakes occur along faults, which are fractures in the Earth's crust where movement has occurred. The focus, or hypocenter, is the point within the Earth where the rupture begins. The epicenter is the point on the surface directly above the focus. Seismic waves radiate from the focus in all directions. Primary waves, or P waves, are compressional waves that travel fastest and can pass through solids, liquids, and gases. Secondary waves, or S waves, are shear waves that travel slower and can only pass through solids. Surface waves travel along the Earth's surface and cause the most damage during an earthquake. The magnitude of an earthquake is a measure of the energy released. The Richter scale, developed by Charles Richter in 1935, is logarithmic, meaning each whole number increase represents a tenfold increase in amplitude and about thirty two times more energy release. The moment magnitude scale is now more commonly used for large earthquakes because it directly measures the seismic moment based on the fault area, slip distance, and rock rigidity. Volcanoes are openings in the Earth's crust through which molten rock, ash, and gases escape. Magma is molten rock beneath the surface, while lava is magma that has reached the surface. Shield volcanoes are broad, gently sloping cones built by the eruption of low viscosity basaltic lava that flows over long distances. Mauna Loa in Hawaii is the largest shield volcano on Earth. Stratovolcanoes, also called composite volcanoes, are steep sided cones built from alternating layers of lava flows, ash, and pyroclastic material. They are associated with subduction zones and produce explosive eruptions. Mount Fuji in Japan and Mount St Helens in the United States are stratovolcanoes. Cinder cones are small, steep volcanoes built from fragments of solidified lava ejected from a single vent. They typically erupt only once. Volcanic eruptions are driven by the buoyancy of magma and the expansion of dissolved gases, primarily water vapor and carbon dioxide. The viscosity of magma controls the style of eruption. Low viscosity basaltic magma allows gases to escape easily, producing relatively gentle effusive eruptions. High viscosity rhyolitic magma traps gases, leading to pressure buildup and explosive eruptions. Pyroclastic flows are fast moving currents of hot gas, ash, and volcanic rock that can travel at hundreds of kilometers per hour and are the most deadly volcanic hazard. Lahars are volcanic mudflows formed when volcanic ash mixes with water from rain or melted snow and ice, rushing down valleys with destructive force. Rocks and minerals are the fundamental components of the Earth. A mineral is a naturally occurring, inorganic, solid crystalline substance with a definite chemical composition and an ordered atomic arrangement. Quartz, feldspar, mica, calcite, and pyroxene are common rock forming minerals. The physical properties used to identify minerals include hardness, measured by the Mohs scale from one for talc to ten for diamond; luster, the appearance of light reflected from the surface; color; streak, the color of the powdered mineral; cleavage, the tendency to break along flat planes; fracture, the pattern of breakage when cleavage is absent; and specific gravity, the density relative to water. Rocks are aggregates of one or more minerals. The three major rock types are igneous, sedimentary, and metamorphic. Igneous rocks form from the cooling and solidification of magma or lava. Intrusive or plutonic igneous rocks cool slowly beneath the surface, allowing large crystals to grow. Granite is a common intrusive igneous rock. Extrusive or volcanic igneous rocks cool rapidly on the surface, producing small crystals or glass. Basalt is the most common extrusive igneous rock. Sedimentary rocks form from the accumulation and lithification of sediments, which can be fragments of preexisting rocks, minerals precipitated from solution, or the remains of organisms. Clastic sedimentary rocks, such as sandstone and shale, are composed of fragments of other rocks. Chemical sedimentary rocks, such as limestone and rock salt, form from the precipitation of minerals from water. Biogenic sedimentary rocks, such as coal and some limestones, form from the accumulation of organic material. Sedimentary rocks often exhibit layering called bedding or stratification, and they may contain fossils, which are preserved remains or traces of ancient life. Metamorphic rocks form when preexisting rocks are subjected to high heat, high pressure, or chemically active fluids, causing solid state changes in mineralogy and texture without melting. Regional metamorphism occurs over large areas during mountain building, producing foliated rocks such as slate, schist, and gneiss, in which platy minerals align perpendicular to the direction of pressure. Contact metamorphism occurs when rocks are heated by an intruding body of magma, producing nonfoliated rocks such as marble from limestone and quartzite from sandstone. The rock cycle describes the continuous transformation between igneous, sedimentary, and metamorphic rocks through processes such as melting, weathering, erosion, deposition, burial, metamorphism, and uplift. Weathering is the breakdown of rocks at or near the Earth's surface. Physical weathering breaks rocks into smaller pieces without changing their chemical composition. Frost wedging, thermal expansion, root growth, and abrasion by wind or water are examples. Chemical weathering alters the chemical composition of rocks through reactions such as dissolution, hydrolysis, and oxidation. Dissolution occurs when water dissolves soluble minerals, such as calcite in limestone, forming caves and karst landscapes. Hydrolysis is the reaction of minerals with water and acids, converting feldspar to clay minerals. Oxidation is the reaction of minerals with oxygen, such as the rusting of iron bearing minerals. Erosion is the removal and transport of weathered material by wind, water, ice, or gravity. Rivers transport sediment as bed load, suspended load, and dissolved load. Glaciers erode through plucking and abrasion, carving U shaped valleys and fjords. Wind erosion produces desert landscapes with arches, hoodoos, and ventifacts. Mass wasting is the downslope movement of rock and soil under the force of gravity, including rockfalls, landslides, debris flows, and slow creep. The geologic time scale divides Earth's history into hierarchical units: eons, eras, periods, and epochs. The Hadean eon, from four point five four to four billion years ago, was a time of intense bombardment, a molten surface, and the formation of the Moon, likely from a giant impact. The Archean eon, from four to two point five billion years ago, saw the formation of the first continental crust, the appearance of the first life in the form of single celled prokaryotes, and the rise of stromatolites built by photosynthetic cyanobacteria that began producing oxygen. The Proterozoic eon, from two point five billion to five hundred forty one million years ago, witnessed the great oxygenation event, which dramatically increased atmospheric oxygen and caused the first mass extinction of anaerobic organisms. The first eukaryotic cells appeared, followed by multicellular life. The Phanerozoic eon, from five hundred forty one million years ago to the present, is divided into three eras. The Paleozoic era, from five hundred forty one to two hundred fifty two million years ago, saw the Cambrian explosion, a rapid diversification of animal life with the appearance of most major phyla in the fossil record. Fish, amphibians, and reptiles evolved. Plants and insects colonized land. The Paleozoic ended with the Permian Triassic extinction event, the most severe mass extinction in Earth history, killing about ninety six percent of marine species and seventy percent of terrestrial vertebrates. The Mesozoic era, from two hundred fifty two to sixty six million years ago, is the age of reptiles, including dinosaurs, pterosaurs, ichthyosaurs, and plesiosaurs. The first mammals and birds appeared during the Mesozoic. Flowering plants, or angiosperms, evolved in the Cretaceous period. The Mesozoic ended with the Cretaceous Paleogene extinction event, caused by a massive asteroid impact, which wiped out non avian dinosaurs and many other groups. The Cenozoic era, from sixty six million years ago to the present, is the age of mammals. Mammals diversified to fill ecological niches left vacant by the extinction of dinosaurs. Primates, including the hominins leading to modern humans, evolved in the Cenozoic. The Quaternary period, which began two point six million years ago, is marked by repeated ice ages, also called glacial cycles, during which large ice sheets advanced and retreated across high latitude regions. Astronomy is the scientific study of celestial objects, space, and the universe as a whole. The observable universe is about ninety three billion light years in diameter and contains an estimated two trillion galaxies. A light year is the distance light travels in one year, approximately nine point four six trillion kilometers. The Milky Way galaxy is the galaxy that contains our solar system. It is a barred spiral galaxy about one hundred thousand light years in diameter and contains an estimated one hundred to four hundred billion stars. The Sun is located about twenty six thousand light years from the galactic center, in a minor spiral arm called the Orion Arm. The Milky Way is part of the Local Group, which includes the Andromeda galaxy, the Triangulum galaxy, and about fifty four other galaxies. Stars are massive, luminous spheres of plasma held together by their own gravity. Nuclear fusion in the core generates energy. In main sequence stars like the Sun, hydrogen fuses into helium via the proton proton chain, releasing energy in the form of photons. The balance between gravitational contraction and the outward pressure from fusion keeps the star stable. Stars are classified by their spectral type, which corresponds to temperature. The sequence from hottest to coolest is O, B, A, F, G, K, M. O type stars are blue, extremely hot, and very massive but rare. M type stars are red, cool, and the most common. The Sun is a G type star. The Hertzsprung Russell diagram plots stellar luminosity against surface temperature. Most stars fall on the main sequence band running from hot, luminous stars to cool, dim stars. Red giants are cool but very luminous, having expanded after exhausting hydrogen in their cores. White dwarfs are hot but very dim, being the dense remnants of low mass stars that have shed their outer layers. The life cycle of a star depends on its mass. Low mass stars like red dwarfs fuse hydrogen slowly and may remain on the main sequence for trillions of years. Medium mass stars like the Sun fuse hydrogen for about ten billion years, then become red giants, then shed their outer layers to form planetary nebulae, leaving behind a white dwarf. High mass stars fuse elements rapidly, progressing through hydrogen, helium, carbon, neon, oxygen, and silicon fusion, eventually producing an iron core. Iron cannot fuse to release energy, so the core collapses, triggering a supernova explosion that blasts most of the star's material into space. The remnant is either a neutron star or a black hole. A neutron star is an extremely dense object, with the mass of the Sun compressed into a sphere only about twenty kilometers in diameter. Pulsars are rapidly rotating neutron stars that emit beams of radiation from their magnetic poles, observed as regular pulses. A black hole is a region of spacetime where gravity is so strong that nothing, not even light, can escape. The boundary around a black hole beyond which no escape is possible is called the event horizon. Stellar mass black holes form from the collapse of massive stars. Supermassive black holes, with millions to billions of solar masses, exist at the centers of most galaxies, including the Milky Way, where Sagittarius A star is the supermassive black hole. Exoplanets are planets that orbit stars other than the Sun. The first confirmed exoplanet orbiting a main sequence star was discovered in 1995. As of 2024, thousands of exoplanets have been discovered. Detection methods include the radial velocity method, which detects wobbles in a star's motion caused by an orbiting planet; the transit method, which detects the dimming of a star as a planet passes in front of it; and direct imaging, which is difficult due to the brightness of the star relative to the planet. The habitable zone, also called the Goldilocks zone, is the region around a star where temperatures are just right for liquid water to exist on the surface of a planet. Planets in the habitable zone are prime targets in the search for extraterrestrial life. The solar system formed about four point six billion years ago from a rotating disk of gas and dust called the solar nebula. The Sun contains ninety nine point eight six percent of the solar system's mass. The four inner planets, Mercury, Venus, Earth, and Mars, are terrestrial planets composed primarily of rock and metal. Mercury is the smallest and closest to the Sun, with extreme temperature variations and no atmosphere. Venus is similar in size to Earth but has a thick carbon dioxide atmosphere with clouds of sulfuric acid, creating a runaway greenhouse effect that makes it the hottest planet, with surface temperatures exceeding four hundred seventy degrees Celsius. Earth is the third planet and the only known place in the universe that supports life. Mars has a thin carbon dioxide atmosphere, Olympus Mons the largest volcano in the solar system, and Valles Marineris a vast canyon system. Evidence suggests that liquid water once flowed on the surface of Mars. The four outer planets, Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus, and Neptune, are gas giants or ice giants. Jupiter is the largest planet, with a composition similar to the Sun, mostly hydrogen and helium. The Great Red Spot is a giant storm that has persisted for centuries. Jupiter has at least ninety two moons, including the four Galilean moons Io, Europa, Ganymede, and Callisto. Saturn is famous for its extensive ring system, made of ice and rock particles. Saturn also has many moons, including Titan, which has a thick nitrogen atmosphere and liquid methane lakes. Uranus rotates on its side, likely due to a giant impact. Neptune is the outermost planet, with the strongest winds in the solar system. The asteroid belt lies between Mars and Jupiter, containing millions of rocky bodies. Ceres is the largest asteroid, now classified as a dwarf planet. The Kuiper belt lies beyond Neptune, containing icy bodies including the dwarf planets Pluto, Eris, Makemake, and Haumea. Pluto, once considered the ninth planet, was reclassified as a dwarf planet in 2006 because it has not cleared its orbital neighborhood of other debris. The Oort cloud is a spherical shell of icy bodies surrounding the solar system at a distance of up to one light year. Comets originate from the Kuiper belt and the Oort cloud. When a comet approaches the Sun, its ices sublimate, forming a glowing coma and sometimes a tail that points away from the Sun due to solar wind and radiation pressure. Cosmology is the study of the origin, evolution, and ultimate fate of the universe. The Big Bang theory is the leading explanation. It states that the universe began as an extremely hot and dense singularity about thirteen point eight billion years ago and has been expanding and cooling ever since. Evidence for the Big Bang includes the observed expansion of the universe, the cosmic microwave background radiation, and the abundance of light elements such as hydrogen and helium. Cosmic microwave background radiation is the afterglow of the Big Bang, filling the universe with a nearly uniform bath of microwaves at a temperature of about two point seven kelvins. The universe is expanding, and the expansion is accelerating. Dark energy is the name given to the unknown force or property of space that drives this acceleration. Dark energy is estimated to constitute about sixty eight percent of the total energy density of the universe. Dark matter is an invisible form of matter that does not emit, absorb, or reflect light. It is detected through its gravitational effects on visible matter, such as the rotation curves of galaxies and gravitational lensing. Dark matter is estimated to constitute about twenty seven percent of the universe. Normal matter, the atoms and molecules that make up stars, planets, and living things, constitutes only about five percent of the universe. The fate of the universe depends on the properties of dark energy. If dark energy remains constant, the universe will continue expanding exponentially, leading to the Big Freeze or heat death, where galaxies move beyond the cosmic horizon, stars exhaust their fuel, and the universe becomes cold, dark, and empty. If dark energy increases, a Big Rip could tear apart galaxies, stars, planets, and eventually atoms. If dark energy reverses, a Big Crunch could cause the universe to collapse back into a singularity. Observational evidence currently favors the Big Freeze scenario. Planetary science applies the principles of geology, astronomy, and physics to study planets, moons, asteroids, comets, and other bodies in the solar system and beyond. Comparative planetology examines similarities and differences among planetary bodies to understand their formation and evolution. Impact cratering is the most common surface process on solid bodies without atmospheres. The Moon is heavily cratered, with maria being dark basaltic plains formed by ancient volcanic eruptions. The Moon's surface is covered with regolith, a layer of fine dust and broken rock. Mars has the largest impact basin, the Hellas Planitia. Mercury also has abundant craters, including the Caloris Basin. Volcanism has shaped the surfaces of Venus, Mars, and Io, which is the most volcanically active body in the solar system due to tidal heating from Jupiter. Tectonics, the deformation of a planetary crust, is seen on Earth, Venus, Mars, and icy moons such as Europa, where linear cracks and ridges indicate a subsurface ocean beneath the ice. Erosion by wind, water, and ice has shaped Earth, Mars, and Titan. Titan has methane rain and rivers that carve channels and fill lakes. The search for life elsewhere in the solar system focuses on environments with liquid water, organic compounds, and an energy source. Europa, Ganymede, Callisto, and Enceladus have subsurface oceans. Enceladus, a moon of Saturn, ejects plumes of water vapor and organic molecules from its south pole, which were sampled by the Cassini spacecraft. Mars shows evidence of past liquid water and may still have subsurface brines. Titan has a methane based hydrological cycle and may have exotic life forms using different biochemistry. The study of meteorites provides direct samples of asteroids and planetary bodies. Stony meteorites, or chondrites, contain chondrules, small spherical inclusions that are among the oldest solid materials in the solar system. Carbonaceous chondrites contain organic compounds, including amino acids, suggesting that the building blocks of life may have been delivered to Earth by meteorites. Iron meteorites come from the cores of differentiated asteroids. Stony iron meteorites contain both silicate minerals and nickel iron alloy. Astrobiology is the interdisciplinary study of the origin, evolution, distribution, and future of life in the universe. It combines biology, chemistry, geology, planetary science, and astronomy. The search for extraterrestrial intelligence, or SETI, uses radio telescopes to listen for artificial signals from other civilizations. The Drake equation estimates the number of communicative civilizations in the galaxy, but most of its terms are highly uncertain. The Fermi paradox asks why we have not yet detected any sign of extraterrestrial intelligence given the vast number of stars and planets. Possible resolutions include that intelligent life is extremely rare, that civilizations tend to destroy themselves, that they are hiding, or that they are communicating in ways we have not yet recognized.
